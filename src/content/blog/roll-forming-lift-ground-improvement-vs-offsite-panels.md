Choose engineered ground works for a roll-forming lift when the resulting production position is technically accepted, available before roofing starts and cheaper across the complete campaign than viable offsite panel supply. Choose offsite panels when their transport and receiving scheme is accepted and avoids enough production-area work to justify the delivered price. Price the ground needed by delivery and lifting equipment in both options; offsite manufacture does not remove that requirement.

This decision belongs in the project budget before equipment ordering. The question is whether to fund a temporary or reusable production position, not whether a crawler track looks suitable for rough ground. The ground engineer defines the enabling works from actual equipment inputs and site conditions. The buyer then compares that completed scope with a genuine alternative supply route.

## Start with two executable roof-supply plans

The onsite plan includes the lift and compatible former, coil supply, approved production locations, receiving crew and the ground works required for those activities. Include installation and later removal of temporary works, inspection arrangements during use and reinstatement of the affected yard. If another trade will reuse the works, allocate only the agreed share to the roofing package and record who pays the remainder.

The offsite plan includes accepted panel lengths and system details, factory production, transport, unloading, storage and delivery to the installation position. Have the roof designer accept any proposed change to panel joints or lengths before estimating it as an equivalent option. A cheaper batch of panels that changes the accepted roof assembly is a redesign proposal, not a like-for-like supply quote.

The roof photograph shows profiled sheets in the foreground and uncovered framing beyond. Use the roof designer's issued drawings to establish which areas and lengths each supply plan must serve. A ground-work saving is only useful when the alternative still delivers the specified roof.

## Separate unavoidable access from production-only works

Put the two site layouts on one plan. Mark the entrance, delivery route and roof receiving points common to both. Then mark the areas used only for the former, coil handling or continuous production. Finally, add any space used only by the offsite alternative, such as panel storage or a separate unloading position.

This prevents a common commercial error: charging all site-access improvements to the forming lift while pricing delivered panels as though a loaded vehicle arrives without needing a suitable route. It also exposes a real advantage where the external supplier can deliver accepted panels to an already assessed handling area and the onsite line would need additional engineered works elsewhere.

Do not derive support works by dividing machine mass by track area. Obtain the offered configuration's travel and production cases and let the ground engineer assess their actual locations. The existing [crawler ground-support guide](/blog/crawler-roll-forming-lift-ground-support-review/) explains that equipment-to-ground interface. This comparison uses the engineer's defined scope and price to choose between complete supply plans.

## Timing can outweigh a narrow equipment-price difference

A technically workable production pad is commercially useless for this roof if its release date falls after the required panel-supply window. Conversely, buying offsite panels does not rescue the programme if approved transport lengths, manufacturing slots and receiving access are unavailable. Put the two supplier programmes beside the main contractor's accepted roof sequence.

Use written dates from the parties that own the work. Allocate investigation and design to the appointed engineer, construction to the ground-works contractor, production to the equipment operator or panel supplier, and receiving to the roofing contractor. Record the consequence of a missed date using the project's actual alternative arrangement. Do not invent a universal daily delay price.

The lowered forming-line package below shows its tracks and deployed supports together. Obtain the configuration-specific inputs for movement to the work location and for production, then budget the engineer's solution for both. Include intermediate positions if the accepted programme requires them.

## Two cost-comparison mistakes

**Mistake 1 — the offsite quote omits the delivery crane's ground works.** In this illustrative comparison, the estimator includes an engineered production area for the lift but records zero enabling cost for finished panels. The delivery plan then introduces a separate crane position outside the assessed area. Add its engineer-defined support and access scope, then compare the totals again. Offsite manufacture changes the site activities; it does not certify their ground.

**Mistake 2 — a temporary pad is priced without its removal.** In a second illustrative budget, the owner requires the yard restored after roofing, but the onsite plan includes only construction of the production area. Add removal, disposal and reinstatement to the package. Credit continued use only when the owner accepts that retained condition and assigns its cost to an actual later activity.

## RFQ attachments that produce a usable comparison

1. The accepted roof panel schedule, material and profile drawings, with any approved alternative lengths identified.
2. A combined site plan showing common delivery access, production positions, storage and roof receiving points.
3. Ground investigation, buried-service and surface records issued to the appointed ground engineer.
4. The proposed lift-and-line configuration and the engineer's required travel and production load-case inputs.
5. The offsite supplier's delivery and unloading plan, including the actual handling equipment and storage requirement.
6. The enabling-works scope, price and release programme from each responsible contractor.
7. The owner's final-ground-condition requirement and any documented reuse or cost-sharing agreement.
8. A full campaign comparison covering materials, equipment, people, transport, ground works and reinstatement.
