Choose a high-level order picker for retrieving stock at rack faces and transferring the picked items to the truck's intended load arrangement. Choose a ceiling maintenance platform when a crew needs an accepted working position at overhead building assets. Combine those purchases only if the manufacturer supports both specific duties in the offered configuration and the warehouse can release the machine for maintenance without losing essential picking coverage.

A warehouse manager sees two people working above floor level and asks whether one capital purchase can serve them both. Start with what each person brings back. The order picker returns with merchandise against a customer order. The maintenance technician returns with a completed inspection, replacement or repair at a named asset. Each needs a different place to stand, a different way to carry items and a different booking window.

## A rack face and an overhead asset have different coordinates

Crown describes the SP high-level order picker around the operator's access to a pick face and transfer of product onto the truck. Its options relate to the offered operator compartment and aisle application. Give the lift-truck bidder the actual rack, stock and order profile, including what the picker takes from a location and where that item goes next. [Crown SP high-level order picker](https://www.crown.com/en-eu/forklifts/order-pickers/high-level-order-picker-sp.html)

For ceiling work, begin with the asset itself. A luminaire over a cross-aisle, an access panel behind a truss and a continuous run of lining require different working positions. Record the task height above the actual supporting floor, the horizontal offset and the room the technician needs to use tools or receive a replacement part. A stock truck that reaches the top pick level can still leave a mechanic on the wrong side of a beam.

The reverse assumption also costs money. A large work deck can be useful for a continuous ceiling run in a cleared bay. Buying it for routine stock picking requires an offered and authorised method for the actual goods, transfers and rack locations. If the quote describes only ceiling and wall access, keep order fulfilment on the warehouse equipment proposal.

## Ask each department for its busiest real shift

From operations, take a representative peak shift with locations, item types, order peaks and required handoff times. From facilities, take a planned service window and one response task that cannot be deferred until the next general shutdown. Put those periods on the same shift plan. The value of a shared purchase depends on the hours when both departments need the machine simultaneously.

A small maintenance workload can be expensive to cover with an owned platform that rarely leaves storage. Where suitable hired access can reach the assets within the actual planned windows, price the booking, delivery, aisle release, return and coverage for an unplanned visit. Where technicians repeatedly need an open bay and the equipment can stay ready for their shift, ownership has a different workload. Give both bidders the same access dates and refund or replacement assumptions drawn from their offers.

## Award the two duties, then decide how many assets to own

For the picker, compare the cost of fulfilling the same basket of orders, including the actual stock handoff at the end of the route. For maintenance, compare the cost of completing the same service visit, including downtime for release of the aisle and any separate part handling. Add ownership, hire or substitute-cover costs for each complete duty. An identical advertised reach is a poor common price denominator because neither contract pays for reaching a height on its own.

There is a useful middle option for a warehouse with uneven maintenance demand. Own the order picker that serves daily dispatch and contract suitable access by service campaign. At renewal, use the actual number of campaigns, short-notice visits and missed access windows to decide whether the next purchase should be an owned personnel platform.

## Two hypothetical purchasing mistakes

**Mistake 1 — Treating stock access as building access.** A buyer counts a light fitting above a rack as covered by a new picker. The service point lies over the back of the rack, while the offered truck presents its operator at the front pick face. Record the service position and request an accepted access proposal for it. Leave the picker's order workload intact.

**Mistake 2 — Making the spare machine disappear at peak time.** A combined offer looks cheaper because maintenance is assigned to weekend mornings. One of those mornings is also the dispatch team's largest replenishment window. Put a quoted substitute, a different maintenance window or dedicated access into the comparison before claiming a saving.

## RFQ attachments for the warehouse and access bidders

- A representative order and rack layout, including pick faces and transfer locations.
- Stock item and load-handling requirements specified by the warehouse operator.
- Facilities asset list with visit frequency, task duration and response windows.
- Section showing the actual supporting floor, asset elevations, offsets and obstructions.
- Site routes, turning areas, floor restrictions and the planned aisle-release procedure.
- Crew and tool schedule plus arrangements for parts removed or installed.
- Demand calendar identifying simultaneous calls and substitute equipment available.
- Separate quotations for picking, personnel access and any expressly offered dual-duty equipment.
