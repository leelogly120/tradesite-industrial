**Buy equipment to relieve the stage that limits accepted panel delivery. A faster former is useful when forming is the constraint; a forming lift is worth comparing when the outlet-to-roof route is the constraint. Neither purchase resolves a roof area that is unavailable for installation.** Start with a short observation of the complete route before asking for a larger package.

This matters for both new lines and existing equipment. The purchasing target is the required panel mix reaching its agreed receiving point during the available work window. A catalogue feed speed measures only one part of that result.

## Follow one order, including its waiting time

Track an identifiable panel order from available coil through accepted production and delivery to the named receiver. If installation is included in your planning target, record that separately as the final stage. Use consistent product references so a delay associated with one profile is not attributed to another.

The forming-equipment manufacturer [Bradbury recommends examining idle time in processes including coil loading, setup and packaging](https://blog.bradburygroup.com/blog/increase-rollforming-production-rate) when investigating throughput. Applying that observation approach to a construction site means including the roof receiver and installation readiness as well. The site extension is a planning inference, not an ARCLIFT performance result.

| Stage to observe | Record for the same order | A purchase or project change to investigate | Buyer entry |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Material ready | Coil available, correct material and time waiting for release | Material ordering or storage arrangement | Material controller: enter coil release time and waiting reason to distinguish supply delays |
| Coil-to-line interface | Waiting reason and available feeding equipment | Defined coil-handling/feed scope | Production lead: record coil-transfer minutes and included feed equipment to identify the upstream gap |
| Setup and product acceptance | Time to first accepted panel and material used | Tooling, training or acceptance support | Quality lead: enter time and material to first accepted panel for the trial product |
| Stable forming | Accepted output and actual running period | Former configuration or line capacity | Production lead: enter accepted length or area and elapsed running time; supplier proposes the matching line |
| Outlet and receiving | Blocked-outlet periods and receiver availability | Ground transfer or elevated-forming arrangement | Receiving supervisor: record blocked-outlet minutes and receiver availability to compare transfer routes |
| Installation handover | Accepted panels received and workface readiness | Receiving coordination or installation resources | Roofing supervisor: enter received quantities and workface release times to identify installation constraints |

Do not add the waiting time of unrelated orders together and label it a machine delay. Note whether each interruption was caused by equipment, missing material, a product decision or the site. That distinction changes what is worth buying.

## Give the production target a usable unit

For a fixed panel profile and width, record accepted panel length per available work period. Where widths differ, accepted panel area can make the product mix clearer. Keep the cut list beside either measure: a total can conceal an unproduced priority zone.

Two simple calculations help organize the enquiry:

**Required average output = accepted panel quantity due ÷ available production window.**

**Observed route output = accepted quantity reaching the agreed receiver ÷ observed window.**

Use the same unit and the same endpoint on both sides. The available window must include the project restrictions you actually expect. Do not subtract every setup or material delay from one figure while leaving those periods inside the other.

These calculations describe a requirement and an observation. Use the actual equipment limits and obtain a project-specific output commitment. Observe a representative panel mix, repeat the observation when the controlling conditions change and retain the reasons for interruptions.

## Three different bottlenecks need different responses

**The line spends time waiting for coil.** First resolve why. A missing coil order calls for a supply decision. Coil available in the wrong place calls for a logistics review. A feed arrangement that cannot address the stated material calls for an equipment proposal. Buying a faster former before separating these causes can leave the original delay untouched.

**The line runs, but the outlet repeatedly waits for the receiver.** Compare the receiving arrangements, including a roof-level forming option where the site geometry supports it. Determine whether the interruption comes from the distance to the roof, panel support, available receiving space or the installation sequence. Moving the outlet only addresses the constraints it actually changes.

**The receiver is ready, but accepted forming output remains insufficient.** Now the line configuration, product mix and setup demands belong at the centre of the enquiry. Ask for a demonstration on the specified material and profile. Capture acceptable output as well as time, since producing more rejected panels does not satisfy the order.

*The roller train is one production stage. Its proposal must match the required profile and material before a rate comparison is meaningful.*

The roller detail belongs beside the last scenario: machine-side improvement matters when the evidence points there. For the other two scenarios, it can be the wrong first investment.

## Run a buying trial with a clear endpoint

Agree which product, material, quantity and receiver the trial covers. Ask the supplier to identify preparation outside the timed observation. Record startup material, accepted panels, rejected panels and interruption reasons without demanding an unsafe continuous run.

For an elevated-forming proposal, establish the tested assembly and its output relationship. A ground demonstration can support a forming-quality question; it does not measure a project's roof receiving performance. Use the supplier trial and site observations together, each for the question it answers.

**Failure case: buying speed into a queue.** A contractor sees panels accumulating at the ground outlet and orders a higher-output line. The transfer resource is still occupied elsewhere, so the queue grows. Address the receiving constraint or compare an appropriate elevated route before increasing the upstream output.

**Failure case: timing only the easy run.** A buyer times a continuous repeat panel but purchases for frequent product changes. Setup and first-panel acceptance consume an unrecorded part of the work window. Include the actual product mix and its change events in the trial brief.

### RFQ attachments for a bottleneck-led purchase

- Approved profile, material and representative panel-length mix.
- Accepted quantity due and the actual production/receiving window.
- Observation log with accepted output and reasons for waiting.
- Existing line, feed and handling-equipment details.
- Roof plan and section with the proposed outlet and receiver.
- Access and setup photographs with measured site constraints.
- Coil specification, material availability and expected change events.
- Destination, power, chassis or carrier scope, and required trial evidence.
