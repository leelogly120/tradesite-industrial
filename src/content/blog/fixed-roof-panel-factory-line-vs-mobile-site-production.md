**Choose a fixed workshop when repeat customers can receive the finished panel lengths and your business can support a central production team. Choose mobile site production when confirmed projects need panels formed near the building and each site can support the production operation.** If most orders suit a workshop but a few need site forming, keep the main line productive at its base and compare a separate mobile service for those exceptions.

This decision starts after you have decided to own production equipment. It is about where that equipment earns its place in the business. A shop serving several customers and a contractor moving between roofs can buy similar forming functions while needing very different people, premises and logistics.

## Decide which orders the production base must serve

A permanent shop is the stronger starting point for a supplier with repeat local orders, a practical delivery radius and room to organise material, forming, inspection and dispatch. Its daily work can continue while installation teams work at separate buildings. The buyer must still verify that finished panels can travel through the delivery route in the required condition and lengths.

Mobile production deserves priority when the awarded work is concentrated on projects where transporting the finished product is the difficult step. The machine, material and team move instead. That can change the delivery problem, but it creates a production location at every site. Access, setup space, utilities, material receipt and the panel receiving arrangement become part of each mobilisation.

Portable forming is an established equipment category: [New Tech Machinery describes making panels at the jobsite](https://newtechmachinery.com/). That category explains the production-location option. The selected ARCLIFT line, base and site arrangement still need their own technical proposal.

## Compare the complete operating model

Fill the last column with actual orders, site information and offers. Use the same approved profile and material basis for both routes so a product change does not distort the comparison.

| Purchase factor | Fixed workshop route | Mobile site route | Buyer entry needed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Repeat demand | Orders share one production base | Work arrives as project mobilisations | Sales lead: enters awarded orders and release dates to locate the repeat workload |
| Finished-panel transport | Delivery route must accept ordered panels | Panels form near the project receiving area | Logistics lead: enters actual panel lengths and accepted delivery/receiving routes to choose production location |
| People | Production team works from the shop | Production team travels or is arranged locally | Production manager: names staff, availability and travel scope to establish an operable route |
| Material | Coil is received and managed centrally | Coil supply follows each production location | Material buyer: enters coil specification, delivery point and storage area for each production location |
| Site resources | Workshop layout and utilities are established | Resources are checked for each building | Site manager: supplies measured layout, power and ground records; supplier returns required interfaces |
| Other customers | Shop can plan separate dispatch orders | Project use ties up the travelling package | Planner: enters orders affected while the line/team is away to price displaced work |
| Equipment quotation | Line and shop interfaces | Line, mobile support and site interfaces | Buyer: lists line, mobile base, feed, transfer and commissioning inclusions to compare complete offers |

The final row matters. A forming line standing in a workshop is not automatically a transportable site package. Conversely, a mobile purchase should not absorb a lift merely because the machine will travel. Elevated forming is appropriate only when the approved panel path calls for it; some projects need a ground-level production arrangement.

## The hidden cost is the work you stop doing

Compare more than equipment and premises. For the workshop route, include the actual panel delivery and handling quotation. For the mobile route, include each mobilisation, the travelling team, site preparation and the work remaining at home while the equipment is away.

Consider a hypothetical supplier whose replacement-panel commitments overlap a remote roof contract. Moving its only line to the remote site leaves those local commitments without production. Compare an actual operated service for the remote roof when it keeps the committed local orders supplied; include that retained work in the cost comparison. Use actual offers to test that choice.

The reverse can happen to a specialist installation contractor. Building a shop around one awarded project can add premises and dispatch work without solving the project's difficult panel delivery route. Choose the mobile package for that project when finished-panel delivery cannot serve the specified lengths and a production team, material route and suitable site position are available.

Do not count unconfirmed future orders as the base workload in either case. Show them separately. A workshop purchase should stand on its credible customer programme; a mobile package should stand on credible project access and mobilisation work.

## Keep a mixed business deliberately mixed

A mixed model works best when each route has a defined job. The workshop can handle repeat deliverable panels, while an identified site-production provider serves exceptional lengths or distant projects. Alternatively, a mobile contractor can buy small replacement batches locally instead of moving an entire production package for them.

The important boundary is product consistency. Use the approved profile, material and acceptance basis across both routes. A panel supplied from the shop and a panel made at the site still need to belong to the same roof specification. Assign responsibility for confirming that before splitting an order.

## Two purchase choices that fail in practice

**Failure case: the factory plan ends at its loading door.** A buyer selects a workshop line using expected production demand, then finds that the actual panel delivery route cannot accommodate the specified finished lengths. The remedy is to resolve delivery and roof-product requirements before committing to that business model. Site forming becomes a real alternative only after its own resources are priced.

**Failure case: the mobile package consumes the shop's only team.** A business plans to serve local orders and a distant site with the same line and crew during overlapping periods. The equipment can occupy only one location. Preserve one service with an actual second production arrangement, or revise the order commitments before purchase.
