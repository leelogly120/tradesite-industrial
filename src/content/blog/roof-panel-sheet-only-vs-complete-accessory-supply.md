Sell roof sheets alone when the customer owns the roof-system design and has a defined route for every matching component. Offer a coordinated accessory package when you can control product compatibility, quantities, supplier responsibility and delivery for the specified roof. Buy specialist accessories before investing in additional manufacturing equipment when the immediate need is a complete order rather than a proven repeat accessory business.

Adding a roll former does not automatically create a complete roofing-system supplier. The commercial decision is whether your business accepts responsibility for a larger, precisely defined supply package. Make that decision before buying equipment or advertising an installed-roof solution.

## State exactly what the customer receives

Write a supply matrix against the customer's roof drawings. Separate formed sheets, folded trims, closures, fixings, clips where the system uses them, sealant products and other specified components. For every item, identify the drawing or product reference, quantity basis, supplying party and compatibility owner. An accessory category name is not an orderable product definition.

Keep the roof designer's role explicit. The supplier provides the agreed products; the responsible designer defines the assembly requirements and accepts substitutions through the project process. A coordinated material delivery is not a claim that the forming machine supplier has designed or certified the whole roof.

## Compare three operating models

The first model is sheet-only manufacturing: produce the controlled panel and transfer the remaining component scope to an identified customer or partner. The second is coordinated supply: manufacture the panel and purchase the associated components from defined sources. The third adds selected accessory manufacture when sustained demand supports its equipment, stock and inspection needs.

Price all three against the same representative orders. Count purchasing, sorting, packaging, shortages, returns and replacement deliveries as well as the component invoice. A larger sales invoice is not evidence of a better margin when the business absorbs small-batch freight and repeated site visits without pricing them.

Keep manufactured and purchased products traceable. A substitute clip, fixing or closure is a product change, even when its description looks similar in a purchase order. Refer proposed changes to the responsible product/design party before supply. Do not let an urgent stock shortage silently alter the roof assembly.

## A buyer-filled package boundary

| Scope decision | Fillable order record and basis | Responsible party | Business decision it supports |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Panel product | Section drawing ___; material ___; revision ___ | Panel product owner | Defines what the former must produce |
| Assembly requirements | Roof detail references ___; specification owner ___ | Customer's roof designer | Defines the components that belong with that panel |
| Quantity schedule | Roof zone ___; piece/item marks ___; take-off revision ___ | Contractor quantity lead | Prevents mixing unrelated or obsolete order lists |
| Bought-in components | Product IDs ___; supplier ___; accepted substitution process ___ | Procurement lead | Establishes a controlled supply route |
| Owned production | Items made in-house ___; operations/equipment ___; inspection method ___ | Workshop lead | Identifies accessory manufacture with a real business case |
| Delivery groups | Zone/date ___; packing list ___; receiving party ___ | Logistics lead | Makes the larger package usable on site |
| Exceptions | Excluded items ___; named provider ___; design interfaces ___ | Commercial lead and customer | Keeps sheet-only and coordinated offers comparable |
| Complete margin | Material ___; buying/admin ___; packing ___; returns ___; delivery ___ | Finance lead | Tests whether the wider scope pays for its actual work |

Every blank should refer to a drawing, order, quotation or named role. “Accessories included” fails as a scope description because it does not say which items or quantities the price covers. Use the table as a commercial attachment, with the same revision in the customer offer and the supplier purchase orders.

## Expand manufacturing only after the supply route works

If bought-in trims or components repeatedly delay otherwise complete orders, measure the cause before buying another machine. Separate slow drawing release from supplier delay, small order economics, stock errors and genuinely missing manufacturing resources. A new machine fixes none of the drawing or quantity problems by itself.

For folded flashings, the [trim-production investment guide](/blog/roof-panel-roll-forming-line-flashing-trim-production/) examines the separate equipment route. For any other component, obtain its own product and manufacturing proposal from the appropriate specialist. Do not treat roof-sheet tooling as a universal accessory production system.

## Two package failures to prevent

**Hypothetical failure case: “complete package” hides an unowned take-off.** The panel producer supplies items from an early drawing while the contractor expects the revised layout. Both parties assume the other has counted edge details. Agree the take-off owner, drawing revision and zone packing list before accepting the larger supply scope.

**Hypothetical failure case: a stock substitution changes the specified interface.** A similar-looking component is sent to keep a delivery date, without the required product acceptance. The installer receives a mixed package and cannot use the planned assembly. Preserve the original product identity and obtain the responsible party's written substitution decision before dispatch.

## Build the supply enquiry around one complete order

Attach these eight inputs to the RFQ:

1. The controlled panel profile and material specification.
2. The roof layout and associated assembly detail references.
3. The marked panel and accessory quantity schedule.
4. Product references for specified bought-in components.
5. A manufacture/buy/customer-supplied responsibility matrix.
6. Zone packing, labelling and delivery requirements.
7. The representative order mix and repeated shortage/cost records.
8. Existing production equipment and the proposed expansion scope.
