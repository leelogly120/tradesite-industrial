Formal training and machine-specific familiarization answer different questions. Training may establish a person's general knowledge and practical ability for a category of equipment. Familiarization then asks whether that person can identify the controls, warnings, limitations, emergency functions and site handover conditions of the particular machine placed in front of them. Neither step alone authorizes work. The employer or site duty holder still needs a controlled decision about competence, supervision, task limits, rescue arrangements and permission to use that equipment in that location. A useful handover record therefore links a named person, an identified machine and revision-controlled information to a defined scope and date. It also records open questions and the events that cancel or reopen the decision. This approach keeps a short demonstration from being mistaken for training, and keeps a certificate from being mistaken for knowledge of every model or site.

For related decisions, see the [remote-control safety planning guide](/blog/remote-control-aerial-platform-safety-planning/), [emergency lowering and rescue plan](/blog/aerial-platform-emergency-lowering-rescue-plan/) and [site acceptance records guide](/blog/crawler-ceiling-platform-site-acceptance-records/). ## Separate training, familiarization and authorization

A buyer should ask who owns each decision before asking how long familiarization will take. Duration is a poor proxy for completeness. A simple machine with a fully experienced user may require a concise review; a different control layout, unusual travel state, accessory, power mode or work environment may require more explanation and supervised practice. The scope must follow the actual gap.

### Distinguish the six control layers

Treat general training, competence assessment, machine-specific familiarization, site induction, task briefing and employer authorization as six linked but non-interchangeable layers. A supplier representative may explain features and provide controlled information, but that does not transfer the employer's responsibility for selecting, supervising and authorizing users. A site briefing may explain traffic or exclusion zones, but it cannot teach an unfamiliar control system. A pre-use check may detect a defect, but it does not prove that the person knows the machine.

The <a href="https://www.hse.gov.uk/construction/safetytopics/mewp.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">UK HSE guidance on mobile elevating work platforms</a> distinguishes training from familiarization on the specific make and model. That is useful planning context for UK work; other destinations need their own legal and contractual review.

### Assign named roles instead of a generic “operator”

Record who supplies controlled machine information, who conducts the familiarization, who assesses remaining gaps, who authorizes use, who supervises the task and who owns the rescue plan. One person can hold more than one role only when the project accepts that arrangement and the person is competent for each role. The handover should also identify who can place the equipment on hold. Ambiguous titles such as “site team” or “supplier” are not enough when a question or defect needs a decision.

### Define the exact authorization boundary

Authorization should state the machine identity, location or work area, task class, allowed operating state, supervision level, validity period and exclusions. It should not imply permission to modify settings, bypass controls, exceed released limits or use unreviewed attachments. If travel, elevation, charging, refuelling, isolation or emergency functions are assigned to different people, say so. The record is stronger when it states what is not authorized.

## Prepare the machine-specific information set

Familiarization needs the controlled information for the identified equipment, not a generic slide deck assembled from similar machines. Confirm the model and unit identifiers, installed options, current operating state, language, document revision and any project-specific limitations before the session starts.

### Verify identity and configuration first

Match the machine, control stations, remote device if fitted, power mode, accessories and safety-related features to the information being used. Record any visible mismatch and stop the affected part of the handover until it is resolved. Do not infer capacity, reach, slope, travel, platform loading, wind, power or emergency behavior from a photograph or neighboring model. Those values belong to the released equipment-specific information and the actual identification plates or records.

### Assemble a controlled reading pack

The pack may include the current operating instructions, warning labels, control identification, limitation summary, inspection and maintenance status, emergency-lowering information, charging or fuel instructions, and the site-approved task and rescue documents. Its exact contents are configuration- and destination-specific. The <a href="https://www.osha.gov/etools/scaffolding/aerial-lifts" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">OSHA aerial-lifts eTool</a> provides U.S. context on trained and authorized workers, retraining triggers, pre-start checks and equipment instructions. It does not define a universal handover form or authorize a machine outside its jurisdiction.

### Plan the physical demonstration safely

Choose a controlled area with enough space, visibility, lighting and separation from unrelated traffic. Confirm the machine state, ground or floor condition and energy boundary required by the approved session plan. The demonstrator should be able to stop the exercise without creating another hazard. A classroom explanation can support the process, but controls, indicators, alarms, interlocks and emergency functions that must be understood should be located on the actual machine or an approved equivalent training arrangement.

### Use a handover readiness checklist

- Identify the machine and installed configuration against controlled records.
- Confirm the user's general training and competence evidence under the employer's system.
- Record the familiarizer, authorizer, supervisor and rescue-plan owner.
- Provide current instructions and labels in a language and format the user can understand.
- Review normal controls, emergency controls, indicators, warnings, limitations and prohibited actions.
- Confirm the intended working height and outreach assumptions against the separate task plan.
- Review roof slope, overhead geometry and any nearby structure as site-briefing inputs.
- Link wind and weather conditions to the site's approved monitoring and hold-point system.
- Confirm ground or floor status through the responsible support review; familiarization does not approve it.
- Confirm the approved work area, access route, exclusion zones and communication method.
- Link the task load, tools and materials to the separate approved load review.
- Record destination compliance and documentation roles without making a generic conformity claim.
- Include transport, building-entry or relocation state only when it affects the machine presented for handover.
- Link emergency lowering and recovery to the site rescue plan rather than improvising a procedure.
- Record questions, unresolved gaps, restrictions and the person responsible for closure.
- Define retraining, refamiliarization and authorization-cancellation triggers.

## Conduct and record the handover

A controlled session should be conversational. The user needs opportunities to point out features, ask questions and demonstrate understanding. A signature collected after a one-way explanation is weak evidence because it does not show which machine, topics or limitations were understood.

### Walk from hazards to controls, not buttons to buttons

Start with the intended task and foreseeable states: access, setup, ground travel, elevation, work position, relocation, isolation and recovery. Then connect each state to the applicable controls, indicators, warnings and limitations. This sequence explains why a control matters. It also exposes site conflicts that a list of button names can miss, such as a remote-control position with poor visibility or an emergency control blocked by stored material.

### Include normal and emergency interfaces

Locate control stations, selector states, enable functions, emergency stops, emergency lowering interfaces, alarms and communication points described by the controlled instructions. Review what the user may do and what must be escalated to a competent technical or rescue role. Do not invent a rescue sequence in the familiarization record. Emergency actions, access and responsible people must match the site-specific rescue plan and equipment information.

### Use demonstration and questions as evidence

Ask the user to identify limitations, select the correct control station, explain warning responses and show the agreed start and stop sequence under supervision. Questions should test decisions, not memorized wording. If the person hesitates or applies knowledge from another model, record the gap and address it before authorization. The <a href="https://www.ipaf.org/en/resource-library/f1-familiarisation-mobile-elevating-work-platforms-mewps" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">IPAF F1 familiarization guidance</a> is useful industry-practice context for reviewing features, functions, limitations, warnings, controls and emergency lowering. It is not a legal approval, training certificate or evidence that a specific session was adequate.

### Make the record auditable but proportionate

Capture the date, place, machine identity, configuration reference, information revision, named participants, topics covered, questions or demonstrations used, restrictions, open items, outcome and authorizing person. Reference the separate training, inspection, task and rescue records instead of duplicating them. A concise record with traceable references is more useful than pages of untailored text.

## Control changes, limitations and trade-offs

The handover is a snapshot. It can cease to support authorization when the user, machine, configuration, information, work area, task, supervision or rescue arrangement changes. The project needs practical triggers that reopen the decision without turning every minor administrative edit into a full repeat session.

### Set clear change triggers

Review familiarization after a different machine or control layout is introduced, an accessory or power mode changes, instructions or warnings are revised, an incident or near miss reveals a gap, the user has not operated the equipment for a period defined by the employer, or the site/task creates unfamiliar conditions. The competent authorizer decides whether the response is a focused update, supervised practice, reassessment, retraining or withdrawal of permission. A supplier cannot make that employer decision from an article.

### Balance concise handover against missing evidence

Excessively generic paperwork wastes attention and may hide the few topics that matter. An undocumented verbal demonstration, however, leaves later teams unable to confirm identity, scope or restrictions. Scale the record to the risk and complexity, but keep the core chain intact: named person, identified machine, controlled information, observed understanding, limitations and authorization. Digital records can improve retrieval; they do not cure poor content or an uncontrolled revision.

<!-- audit-section: limitations-not-fit -->
### Recognize when familiarization is not enough

This process does not fit a person who lacks required general training, competence or language support, and it cannot cure missing machine information, defects, an unapproved task, unsuitable site conditions or an absent rescue plan. It is not a maintenance instruction, inspection, test or technical acceptance. If identity, controls, safety functions or authorization responsibility remain unclear, hold use and escalate through the employer's approved system.

### Request a handover package that can be reviewed

Provide the equipment identity, intended task, working height, work zone, user roles, training evidence status, preferred language, site and floor conditions, destination requirements, transport or access state, rescue-plan owner and known configuration differences through a controlled project channel. Ask for the current equipment-information index, familiarization topics, open-input list and record template before delivery or mobilization. The final employer authorization must remain project- and person-specific. ## FAQ

#### Is familiarization the same as operator training?

No. Training may cover a category of equipment and general competence. Familiarization addresses the controls, features, warnings and limitations of the particular machine, while employer authorization remains a separate decision.

#### Who should sign the familiarization record?

The record should identify the person who conducted the session, the participant and the competent employer or site role that authorizes use. Exact signatures and retention rules depend on the applicable system and destination.

#### Must familiarization be repeated for every machine?

Not automatically in the same form. A competent reviewer should compare the new machine, configuration, controls, instructions, task and site conditions, then decide what focused familiarization or reassessment is necessary.

#### Can a supplier representative authorize an employee to operate?

A representative may provide information or conduct an agreed familiarization, but employer permission, supervision and task authorization stay with the responsible employer or site duty holder unless a valid project arrangement explicitly assigns those roles.
