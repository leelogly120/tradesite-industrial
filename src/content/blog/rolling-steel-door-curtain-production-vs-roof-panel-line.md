**Choose a dedicated rolling-door slat business when customers will buy the specified compatible slats and you can supply their required finishing and interface details. For complete-door orders, fund the curtain assembly and coordinated door components as well. Choose roof-panel production when the contracted output is a released roof profile and its required accessories.** A roof-panel roll former is not a general machine for manufacturing either finished product.

Both businesses begin with metal and shaped profiles, but their sale units differ. A roof order can require sheets allocated to roof positions and coordinated junction parts. A rolling steel door order can require a curtain assembled from many interlocking slats, together with the other parts of the closure. Decide which product the customer accepts before comparing line prices or output rates.

## Start with the acceptance unit on the sales order

Write one representative order from enquiry through delivery. Identify whether you sell slat lengths to a door manufacturer, assembled curtains to an integrator, complete doors, or roofing sheets to a contractor. The same amount of shaped metal can support very different quantities of accepted saleable product. Use the customer's actual drawings and acceptance documents, not a general metalworking capability statement.

[Dallan's roller-shutter line description](https://www.dallan.com/en/products/rollformers/roller-shutter-doors/) distinguishes dedicated slat profiles and downstream machining requirements. Its separate [curtain-assembly technology](https://www.dallan.com/en/technologies/production-technology/coil-to-window/) illustrates the additional production step between formed profiles and an assembled curtain. Those are examples of a specialised process family, not capabilities assigned to an ARCLIFT roof-panel machine.

For a slat-supply business, identify the customer who takes responsibility for the assembled door. Obtain the required section, material, finish, end details and compatibility requirements in that commercial arrangement. Price the agreed accepted component, with the rejected-piece and replacement arrangements visible. If the customer buys complete doors, a bundle of correct slats is still an unfinished delivery.

## A curtain is one part of the door package

[Cookson's rolling-door component guide](https://www.cooksondoor.com/architect-tools-and-resources/rolling-door-101) identifies the curtain, guides, support brackets and counterbalance assembly as separate parts of its door system. That establishes why the complete closure requires more than shaped strip. The actual door designer and product authority define the compatible parts and acceptance requirements for your chosen product.

For a complete-door proposal, identify which assemblies you manufacture, which you purchase and who coordinates the system. Include the actual bottom member, end arrangements, guides, support and operating package specified by that product. Keep product-specific qualification and documentation in the responsible supplier's scope. Do not borrow fire, wind or cycle claims from a different manufacturer's complete door.

For a roof-panel proposal, define the accepted panel section, material, finish, cut lengths and associated roof details. Use the released roof system's requirements for production trials and the finished supply package. The machinery buyer then prices the forming process that produces those pieces; it does not convert a general workshop photo into evidence of system approval.

A mixed business can buy complete door sets while manufacturing its accepted roof panels. It can also supply only the defined slat component to an established door producer. Keep each revenue stream tied to the output that its actual customer buys. Owning an assembly operation is justified by accepted orders and its complete cost, rather than by the appearance of another formed-metal product.

## Compare output after the unfinished work is included

Translate the quoted equipment output into the unit the customer accepts. For curtain supply, include the specified slat quantities, associated details, assembly and delivery identification. For sheets, include the accepted lengths, required finishing and the delivery quantity the roofer receives. Use actual orders and process proposals; do not infer whole-door output from a line's linear speed.

Ask the equipment supplier for a demonstration or trial against your actual product and material. Give the accepting party its required sample and measurement records. The acceptance boundary must include the mating features relevant to the component, not only a visually straight sample. A profile that looks similar still needs the product authority's actual acceptance.

The coil-processing photograph below shows another separate process boundary. Purchased coil, preparation equipment and the forming cell each need their own specification. Locate any required preparation or bought-in service in the costed process rather than assuming it is included in the former's sale amount.

## Two metalworking investments with an incomplete product

**Mistake: a slat-output quotation is counted as complete-door capacity.** A hypothetical buyer budgets a dedicated former but leaves curtain assembly, purchased operating parts and the system acceptance outside the plan. Rebuild the investment around the accepted customer product. Buy a component-only line only where the customer actually accepts that component and owns the remaining integration.

**Mistake: a roof line is expected to change into a door factory through a new profile name.** Another business offers door curtains before obtaining an attributable tooling, finishing and assembly proposal. Keep the roof line on its supported product scope. Purchase the specialist process package or source accepted complete doors until that separate business has a real production and acceptance route.
