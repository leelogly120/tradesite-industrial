# Pick and Carry vs Spider Lift: Which Is Right for Your Project?

Choosing between a pick and carry crane and a spider lift is one of the most common equipment decisions contractors face when a project involves both lifting heavy loads and working at height. These two machine categories overlap in some applications but diverge sharply in design philosophy, capability profile, and total cost of ownership. Making the wrong choice can mean overspending on mobilization, underutilizing machine capacity, or compromising safety on site.

This guide provides a side-by-side comparison grounded in real specifications, industry standards, and field experience. Whether you are a rental fleet manager evaluating procurement options or a project engineer selecting equipment for a specific job, the analysis below will help you match the right machine to your work.

## What Is a Pick and Carry Crane?

A pick and carry crane is a compact, self-propelled crane designed to lift a load and carry it while in motion — something conventional mobile cranes cannot do safely. The machine travels with the boom raised and the load suspended, allowing the operator to transport materials across the job site without setting up and tearing down rigging at each stop.

Most pick and carry cranes use a boom telescope mechanism with hydraulic extension, offering maximum lifting capacities from 3 tonnes to over 25 tonnes. The chassis is typically rubber-tired with a tight turning radius, enabling operation in confined industrial environments such as factories, warehouses, shipyards, and power plants. Popular configurations include counterweight-integrated designs and pick-and-carry models with outriggers for stationary heavy lifts.

Key performance metrics for a mid-range pick and carry crane include a maximum boom length of 20–30 meters, a tip height of approximately 25–35 meters, and a rated capacity at full reach of 1–3 tonnes. Drive speeds typically range from 3–8 km/h under load. The machine's compact footprint — often under 2 meters wide and under 3 meters tall with the boom stowed — allows access through standard industrial doorways and between tight racking layouts.

Industries that rely heavily on pick and carry cranes include steel fabrication (moving heavy beams), precast concrete installation, HVAC mechanical room fit-out, shipbuilding, and bridge construction. The ability to lift, drive, and place a load in a single continuous operation eliminates the need for additional transport equipment on site.

For projects that also require elevated personnel access, a pick and carry crane alone is not sufficient — it is a material-handling machine, not an aerial work platform. That is where spider lifts enter the comparison.

## What Is a Spider Lift?

A spider lift — formally known as a tracked aerial work platform or compact crawler platform — is a self-propelled elevated work platform mounted on articulated rubber tracks. It is purpose-built to lift personnel and materials to working height, with platform heights ranging from 15 meters to over 50 meters in the largest models.

The defining feature of a spider lift is its compact stowed profile combined with significant outreach. A typical 20-meter spider lift stows to approximately 0.8 meters wide and 2 meters tall, allowing it to pass through a single doorway, travel through narrow corridors, and operate on sites where conventional boom lifts cannot fit. Once on location, hydraulic outriggers deploy to create a stable working base, and the multi-section boom extends to deliver the platform to the required height and outreach.

Spider lifts operate on rubber tracks that distribute ground pressure to as low as 3–5 psi, making them suitable for sensitive surfaces such as finished floors, marble terraces, and green roofs. Many models offer dual power options — a diesel engine for outdoor rough-terrain work and an electric motor for indoor or emission-free operation. The [ARCLIFT crawler lift series](/products/crawler-lifts) exemplifies this category, offering working heights from 15 to 42 meters with lithium-electric and hybrid powertrains.

Applications for spider lifts span building facade maintenance, window and curtain wall installation, bridge inspection, tree surgery, stadium and arena maintenance, church and heritage building restoration, and industrial plant shutdowns. The combination of low ground pressure, compact access dimensions, and significant working height makes spider lifts the default choice when the primary task is getting people and tools to elevation safely and efficiently.

## Capability Comparison: Lifting vs Accessing Height

The fundamental difference between these two machine types is their primary function. A pick and carry crane is optimized for **moving heavy loads horizontally** while maintaining lift capacity. A spider lift is optimized for **delivering personnel and light materials vertically** to a working platform at height.

| Parameter | Pick and Carry Crane | Spider Lift |
|-----------|---------------------|-------------|
| Primary function | Material lifting and transport | Personnel access at height |
| Maximum lift capacity | 3–25+ tonnes | 200–400 kg (platform load) |
| Maximum working height | 25–35 m (hook height) | 15–50+ m (platform height) |
| Carries load while moving | Yes — core design feature | No — must stow boom to travel |
| Personnel lifting | Not rated for people (unless certified) | Designed and certified for personnel |
| Ground pressure | 15–30 psi (rubber tires) | 3–8 psi (rubber tracks) |
| Minimum access width | 1.5–2.0 m | 0.7–1.2 m |
| Terrain capability | Flat, prepared surfaces | Rough terrain, slopes up to 20° |
| Setup time | 5–15 minutes | 5–15 minutes |

A pick and carry crane can place a 5-tonne HVAC unit on a rooftop faster than any spider lift because that is what it is designed to do — lift heavy, move, and place. A spider lift can deliver two technicians with tools to a point 30 meters above ground on a narrow facade with zero risk of load swing, because that is its design purpose.

The overlap zone exists in lighter-duty applications: installing precast panels, placing mechanical equipment, or positioning structural steel where loads are under 1 tonne and heights are moderate. In these scenarios, project managers sometimes stretch a spider lift beyond its intended use by rigging loads from the platform — a practice that violates manufacturer load ratings and OSHA/EN standards for aerial work platforms. Conversely, using a pick and carry crane to suspend a work basket for personnel violates ANSI B30.5 and EN 13000 unless the crane is specifically equipped and certified for man-riding.

The correct approach: if the task is lifting and placing materials, specify a pick and carry crane. If the task is working at height from a platform, specify a spider lift. If both tasks exist on the same site, use both machines — the mobilization cost of a second unit is far less than the safety and productivity cost of misusing one.

## Cost Analysis: Ownership and Rental

Understanding the true cost of each machine type requires looking beyond the sticker price or daily rental rate. The total cost equation includes acquisition or rental cost, operator requirements, transport, fuel, maintenance, and utilization rate.

**Purchase cost**: A new pick and carry crane in the 6–10 tonne class typically costs $120,000 to $250,000, while larger 20+ tonne units range from $300,000 to $600,000. Spider lifts range from $80,000 for a 15-meter electric model to $400,000+ for a 50-meter diesel unit. On a per-meter-of-height basis, spider lifts deliver more vertical reach per dollar invested.

**Rental rates**: Weekly rental for a mid-range pick and carry crane (8–12 tonnes) runs approximately $2,000–$4,000, while a 20-meter spider lift rents for $1,500–$3,500 per week. The spider lift is generally more cost-effective for height-access-only projects, while the pick and carry crane offers better value for mixed lifting and transport tasks.

**Operator costs**: Both machines require a single certified operator. Pick and carry crane operators typically hold a crane operator license (NCCCO in the US, CPCS in the UK). Spider lift operators require IPAF certification for mobile elevated work platforms (MEWPs). Hourly rates for both range from $28–$45 depending on region and experience. See our [aerial platform rental guide](/blog/aerial-platform-rental-guide) for detailed cost breakdowns by region.

**Transport**: Both machines are compact enough to transport on a standard flatbed trailer. A typical 20-meter spider lift weighs 4,000–7,000 kg; a mid-range pick and carry crane weighs 8,000–15,000 kg. Heavier pick and carry cranes may require a low-loader, adding $500–$1,500 to mobilization costs.

**Fuel and maintenance**: Diesel-powered pick and carry cranes consume 8–15 liters of fuel per hour under load. Spider lifts with dual power systems consume 3–8 liters per hour in diesel mode and draw 5–15 kW in electric mode. Maintenance intervals for both machines are typically 250–500 operating hours, with annual costs running 3–5% of purchase price.

## Safety Standards and Compliance

Safety compliance is non-negotiable for both machine types, but the regulatory frameworks differ because one machine handles loads and the other carries people.

**Pick and carry cranes** fall under crane safety standards. In the United States, OSHA 1926 Subpart CC governs crane operation, and ASME B30.5 covers mobile crane design and inspection. The European equivalent is EN 13000 for mobile cranes. Key requirements include annual load testing, monthly inspections by a competent person, operator certification, and load chart compliance at all operating radii. Pick and carry operations add a dynamic load consideration — the crane must remain stable while traveling with a suspended load, which is why manufacturers specify maximum travel speeds and grade limits under load.

**Spider lifts** comply with aerial work platform standards. ANSI A92.20 (design) and A92.22 (safe use) govern MEWPs in North America. EN 280 covers design requirements in Europe. These standards mandate load sensing, tilt protection, emergency descent systems, platform guardrails, and wind speed limitations. The IPAF (International Powered Access Federation) provides operator training and certification recognized across 80+ countries.

Both machine types require pre-use inspections, documented maintenance records, and site-specific risk assessments before operation. The key safety distinction: a pick and carry crane operator sits in a cab and controls the load from below; a spider lift operator stands in a platform at height with fall protection and secondary guarding systems in place. This fundamental difference in operator position drives the equipment design, certification path, and site safety planning.

## Site Conditions and Environmental Factors

Job site conditions often determine which machine is viable, regardless of task requirements. Terrain, access constraints, surface sensitivity, and environmental regulations all play a role.

**Ground conditions**: Pick and carry cranes require firm, level surfaces — typically compacted gravel, concrete, or asphalt. Their higher ground pressure (15–30 psi) limits operation on soft ground, green roofs, or finished floors without protection mats. Spider lifts excel here with ground pressures as low as 3 psi on rubber tracks, allowing operation on grass, pavers, sensitive roofing membranes, and indoor floors. For projects on [sensitive surfaces or low-ground-pressure requirements](/blog/low-ground-pressure-aerial-lift), the spider lift is the clear winner.

**Access dimensions**: Spider lifts routinely pass through 0.8-meter-wide doorways and navigate corridors with 2-meter ceiling clearance. Pick and carry cranes need at least 1.5–2.0 meters of width and 2.5–3.0 meters of height to operate. Interior renovation projects, heritage buildings, and occupied facilities almost always favor spider lifts for this reason.

**Outdoor performance**: Both machines handle outdoor conditions, but pick and carry cranes are more sensitive to wind loads due to their taller boom profiles and suspended loads. Spider lifts with wind rating up to 12.5 m/s (Beaufort 6) can operate in moderate outdoor conditions. Pick and carry cranes typically limit operation to wind speeds below 9.8 m/s (Beaufort 5) when the boom is raised.

**Indoor air quality**: In enclosed or semi-enclosed spaces, emissions matter. Electric spider lifts produce zero exhaust emissions and operate at noise levels below 75 dB(A), making them suitable for occupied buildings, hospitals, and food processing facilities. Diesel pick and carry cranes produce 95–105 dB(A) and require ventilation when operating indoors.

## When to Choose Each Machine

Use a **pick and carry crane** when:

- The primary task is lifting and placing heavy materials (2+ tonnes)
- Loads must be transported horizontally across the job site
- Work is on prepared, level surfaces with adequate access width
- The project involves steel erection, precast installation, or heavy mechanical equipment placement
- Single-lift operations where speed of placement matters more than personnel access

Use a **spider lift** when:

- The primary task is personnel access at height (15+ meters)
- Work occurs on sensitive surfaces, uneven terrain, or in confined spaces
- Indoor operation requires zero emissions and low noise
- The project involves facade work, glazing, painting, or inspection
- Multiple work positions at height are needed across a large site
- Heritage or occupied building access is required

For complex projects that involve both heavy lifting and elevated work — such as a full building envelope replacement or an industrial plant shutdown — the optimal solution is to mobilize both a pick and carry crane for material handling and a spider lift for personnel access. The combined daily operating cost of two machines is typically 20–30% less than the productivity loss and safety risk of forcing one machine to do both jobs.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a spider lift be used for lifting heavy materials?

Spider lifts are designed for personnel access and light material handling, with typical platform load capacities of 200–400 kg. While you can transport tools, light fixtures, and small equipment in the platform, they are not rated for heavy lifting. For loads exceeding 500 kg, a pick and carry crane or telehandler is the appropriate choice.

### Can a pick and carry crane lift people?

A pick and carry crane can only lift personnel if it is specifically equipped and certified for man-riding operations under standards such as EN 13000 Annex ZA or ASME B30.5 with a personnel platform attachment. Standard pick and carry cranes without this certification must never be used to suspend a work basket. Violations carry severe OSHA penalties and create extreme safety hazards.

### Which machine is better for indoor use?

Spider lifts with electric power are far superior for indoor use. They produce zero emissions, operate below 75 dB(A), and have compact dimensions that navigate doorways and corridors. Pick and carry cranes are generally too large and too loud for indoor environments and require diesel exhaust ventilation if used in enclosed spaces.

### How do I transport a spider lift or pick and carry crane to the job site?

Both machines transport on standard flatbed trailers. A 20-meter spider lift typically weighs 4,000–7,000 kg and loads with a ramp or small crane. A mid-range pick and carry crane weighs 8,000–15,000 kg and may require a heavier trailer. Many rental companies include delivery and pickup in their contracts for projects within a 100 km radius.

### What certifications do operators need?

Pick and carry crane operators need a crane operator license — NCCCO certification in the US, CPCS/NPORS card in the UK, or equivalent national credentials. Spider lift operators need IPAF PAL card certification for the specific machine category (typically 3b for tracked booms). Both require site-specific induction and a valid medical fitness certificate.

## Conclusion

The pick and carry crane and the spider lift are complementary, not competing, machines. Each excels in its design domain: the pick and carry crane for heavy load handling and transport, the spider lift for safe, efficient personnel access at height. Project success depends on matching the machine to the task — or deploying both when the scope demands it.
