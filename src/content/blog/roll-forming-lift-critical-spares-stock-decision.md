Stock an identified spare locally when its absence stops a committed job, the documented replenishment route is longer than the available recovery window, and a competent maintenance arrangement can put the part into service. Order on demand when an applicable item has dependable local supply or no justified stocking case. Begin with the configuration-specific parts list and failure history, not a large mixed box described as essential spares.

This decision concerns readiness spending after the equipment and support boundaries are known. The [spares and support scope guide](/blog/roll-forming-lift-maintenance-spares-supply/) identifies the responsible providers. Here the buyer decides which confirmed items to hold, where to hold them and how to maintain a usable stock.

The tooling close-up below illustrates the need for exact identity. A component belongs to a particular geometry and assembly. A photograph can locate the item, but dimensions, revision and the applicable part record determine what procurement orders.

## Classify parts by consequence and recovery route

Use three practical categories. First are scheduled consumables or service items with usage and replacement requirements in the applicable maintenance documents. Their stocking plan follows the actual service programme and replenishment route. Second are critical items whose absence prevents the committed duty and for which recovery cannot fit the project window. Third are items that are economical to source after diagnosis because supply and recovery arrangements fit the owner's needs.

Do not assign a category from price alone. An inexpensive item can prevent a complete package from working, while an expensive spare can remain unused as equipment revisions change. Equally, high failure consequence does not prove that a particular component will fail often. Keep consequence, observed failure evidence and replacement applicability as separate inputs.

Where the fleet has no relevant history, use the equipment provider's documented service requirements and actual supplier offers to build an initial plan. Mark that plan as a purchasing assumption and review it against subsequent usage. Do not turn an invented failure rate into a precise stock quantity.

## A stock decision record for each candidate item

| Stock input | Buyer field | Owner and evidence basis | Decision use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Applicability | Machine, assembly, part and revision: ___ | Equipment provider and fleet engineer; applicable parts record | Excludes incompatible or obsolete items |
| Failure consequence | Duty lost and affected booked projects: ___ | Operations planner; actual equipment dependency | Identifies the operational exposure |
| Usage evidence | Service requirement or recorded replacements: ___ | Maintenance manager; manual and maintenance history | Supports the expected demand |
| Replenishment | Written supply route and current delivery estimate: ___ | Procurement; dated supplier and transport offers | Compares outside supply with the recovery window |
| Local readiness | Diagnostic, fitting and release resources: ___ | Maintenance manager; competent service arrangements | Tests whether stock actually shortens recovery |
| Shared use | Applicable machines and simultaneous demand: ___ | Fleet engineer and scheduler; configuration records | Determines whether central stock serves several assets |
| Storage | Conditions, shelf life and inspection requirements: ___ | Parts provider; item-specific instructions | Prices preservation and replacement of ageing stock |
| Commercial choice | Purchase, holding and recovery alternatives: ___ | Buyer; comparable actual quotations | Chooses local stock, central stock or on-demand supply |

Complete the record for the specific item, not only for a category such as hydraulics or electrics. When a provider offers a kit, require the contents and quantities to be itemised. That lets the owner remove duplicates, identify missing coverage and keep revisions traceable.

## A spare on the shelf is only one recovery resource

The recovery period includes diagnosis, correct-item identification, supply, competent work and the required return-to-service checks. Local stock shortens the supply part of that sequence. It does not replace the other stages. If qualified attendance is the longest unavailable resource, buying more parts alone leaves the main delay unchanged.

Great Britain's [HSE maintenance guidance](https://www.hse.gov.uk/work-equipment-machinery/maintenance.htm) stresses planning and competent maintenance arrangements. For this purchase, name the responsible service resources alongside the proposed stock. The article provides no repair steps or authority to substitute a component.

Central stock works when the identified part is genuinely shared across the fleet and the transfer route fits the jobs. Place a dedicated stock at a remote project when the full transfer and replenishment route defeats that recovery requirement. A shared part should not be counted as immediately available to two simultaneous projects after it has already been allocated to one.

The yard photograph below shows a forming package as a combination of assemblies. Its spare plan should cover the actual owned combination, including separately sourced equipment, without assuming one part list serves every blue former or red lifting structure.

## Two stock purchases that fail to create readiness

**Failure 1 — a common-looking part belongs to another revision.** In this illustrative case, a fleet orders one stock item for several machines because the housings look alike. The maintenance team later finds different applicable part records. Correct the decision by mapping the proposed spare to each machine before buying a shared quantity. Treat provider-approved supersessions as recorded changes, not informal substitutions.

**Failure 2 — the part arrives but the service resource does not.** A remote contractor holds a spare but has no competent arrangement for diagnosis and return to service. The project remains interrupted. Correct the purchase by budgeting the complete recovery route and comparing local stock with an actually available service package.

### Eight RFQ attachments for a justified spare package

1. Proposed equipment combination and existing machine identities where applicable.
2. Configuration-specific parts and assembly references available to the owner.
3. Planned service schedule and relevant recorded replacement history.
4. Committed project calendar and the duties affected by an unavailable assembly.
5. Current supplier, transport and local sourcing offers for candidate items.
6. Competent diagnosis, maintenance and return-to-service arrangements at the destination.
7. Storage locations and item-specific preservation requirements.
8. The completed stock worksheet with shared-fleet applicability and requested quantities.
