Group short roof-panel orders into compatible production runs when their approved profile, material, finish and delivery windows allow it. Keep an order separate when grouping delays its required installation sequence, mixes incompatible inputs or creates finished panels without a usable receiving plan. Choose the schedule that completes accepted customer orders on time, rather than the schedule that gives the former its longest uninterrupted run.

This is a sequencing decision for released work. The [coil-versus-finished-panel inventory guide](/blog/roof-panel-roll-forming-coil-vs-finished-panel-inventory/) addresses what to hold before orders are released. Here the orders already exist; the planner must decide which work goes first and whether combining it creates a real saving.

The row of rollers below illustrates a physical forming arrangement. Keep a production group inside the approved tooling and material envelope. A common colour or similar-looking profile is not enough to treat orders as interchangeable.

![A long row of silver forming rollers sits in a blue frame on a workshop floor.](/images/equipment/buyer-20261102-roller-row.webp)
*Group work by the approved product and actual change requirements, not by the appearance of the finished sheet.*

## Build a compatibility group before moving dates

Start with the released profile drawing and material specification for each order. Include coating, finish, effective cover, length basis and any order-specific acceptance criteria. Then establish which changes require a different material issue, tooling arrangement, setup or inspection step under the line's actual operating documents.

Separate this technical compatibility decision from commercial priority. Two orders can be compatible to manufacture yet need different release or delivery dates. Conversely, orders for the same customer can require different inputs. Keep both views in the schedule so that a convenient machine sequence does not silently change the agreed product or dispatch requirement.

Use recorded changeover and inspection periods from the relevant operation, or a clearly identified planning estimate when records do not yet exist. There is no universal changeover time to insert into every roof-panel quotation. Evaluate any equipment option against the buyer's actual repeated change pattern.

## Compare complete orders on a planning sheet

| Scheduling input | Buyer entry | Owner and measurement basis | Rule for the proposed run |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Released product | Profile revision, material and finish: ___ | Technical office; approved production documents | Group only technically compatible work |
| Accepted quantities | Panel identifiers and required quantities: ___ | Order administrator; released customer order | Keep each order's accepted balance separate |
| Change requirements | Material, setup and inspection steps: ___ | Production engineer; applicable line instructions and records | Count the actual work removed by grouping |
| Required delivery | Complete-order or installation-zone dates: ___ | Customer coordinator; accepted delivery programme | Prevent one job's saving from delaying another |
| Available resources | Former, crew, handling and packing slots: ___ | Production planner; shared resource calendar | Schedule the complete route, not only the former |
| Holding space | Bundle lengths, locations and retrieval order: ___ | Warehouse lead; measured storage and movement layout | Reject groups that create inaccessible stock |
| Identification | Panel, order, revision and bundle method: ___ | Quality and dispatch leads; traceability plan | Preserve identity through mixed production |
| Comparison cost | Setup, handling, holding and disruption costs: ___ | Buyer; the same accepted delivered quantities | Compare the whole order sequence |

Do not hide incomplete orders inside a total-metres figure. A large quantity of accepted panels is not a complete dispatch if a required short item is missing. Show the remaining balance and the next feasible completion slot for each customer order.

## An illustrative three-order decision

Consider three released jobs: A and C use the same approved profile and material, while B needs a different approved arrangement. A is required first. B must leave before C, and the packing area cannot hold C without blocking B's retrieval. A long combined A-and-C run removes a change but creates a dispatch problem.

Choose A, then B, then C when that sequence meets the committed dates and the holding constraint cannot be removed economically. Group A and C only if their shared run still leaves a supported route for B: sufficient time for B's change and production, available packing resources, and a storage location that preserves B's access. Price those extra arrangements before declaring the combined run cheaper.

This example uses no assumed machine rate. Populate the sheet with the site's own periods and costs. If grouping saves a recorded setup period but adds more handling, storage or delay cost, the production grouping has failed its commercial purpose.

## Preserve the installation order after production grouping

For long roof panels, identify the building zone and receiving sequence as well as the manufacturing order. If the customer installs by roof zone, a bundle mixed across zones can force additional sorting at the project. The production saving must include that consequence wherever the seller's supply scope carries it.

The roof photograph below shows long continuous panel lines. That installed layout gives the dispatch plan a practical endpoint: send the identified panels needed for the next released work area, rather than merely the next pieces made by the line.

![Long straight seam lines extend across a metal roof towards the horizon.](/images/equipment/buyer-20261101-roof-panels.webp)
*Use the roof-zone schedule to define panel and bundle identity before combining workshop orders.*

Document a small urgent-order rule. An authorised planner should compare the requested interruption with the jobs it displaces, the added change requirements and the revised dispatch dates. Sales staff should not promise an immediate run without that comparison. A controlled exception is more useful than a nominally efficient plan that is rewritten informally throughout the day.

## Two mistakes that hide the true bottleneck

**Mistake 1 — same colour is treated as the same material.** In this illustrative case, two orders look alike but have different approved material or finish requirements. The correction is a compatibility group built from the released specifications. Keep the orders separate unless the responsible technical process establishes a common approved input.

**Mistake 2 — the former finishes early but dispatch finishes late.** Grouping produces a large early bundle that blocks retrieval of the urgent order. The correction is a complete production, packing and retrieval schedule. Do not purchase another former to solve a queue created by the current storage sequence.
