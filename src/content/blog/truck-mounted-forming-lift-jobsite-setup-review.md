A truck-mounted roll-forming lift raises a compatible roof-panel forming machine so its outlet can serve a planned receiving level. At the jobsite, the important layout question is how the truck, lifting structure, forming line, coil supply and roof receiving area fit together. A truck can reach the building and still face the wrong direction for production, occupy the delivery lane or leave its proposed support area over an unresolved underground feature.

Start with the panel outlet and work backwards through the equipment to the ground layout. Then test that arrangement against delivery traffic and the construction programme. This guide explains that physical coordination. The [chassis integration review](/blog/truck-mounted-roll-forming-chassis-interface-review/) covers equipment-to-vehicle matching, while the [roof-panel handover guide](/blog/elevated-roll-forming-roof-panel-handover-zones/) develops the receiving interface. The [truck-mounted roll-forming lift product reference](/products/arc-t18-truck-mounted-roll-forming-lift/) introduces the equipment family; the final configuration still needs project-specific confirmation.

## Start with the forming outlet and receiving area

### Identify the machine being raised

The lifting equipment and the roll former have different jobs. The lift positions the compatible forming equipment; the forming line shapes the specified material into the intended panel. The truck provides a vehicle architecture for the integrated arrangement. A jobsite sketch needs to show all three because a rectangle labelled “truck” conceals the forming outlet, attachments and work-state geometry that determine the useful position.

Ask the equipment party for the proposed arrangement in the states relevant to the layout. Avoid starting with an unrelated truck outline and adding a nominal height. The [roll-forming line specification guide](/blog/roll-forming-line-specification-long-span-roof-panels/) explains the line information that must accompany the lifting concept.

### Place the outlet against a real roof reference

Mark the intended roof receiving position on a plan and elevation. Identify its datum, the forming outlet reference and the direction in which the panel would leave the line. Eave height alone does not establish the outlet requirement: the receiver may be at a different level or offset from the eave, and the forming machine occupies space above its supporting structure.

This step can expose a layout problem before anyone discusses a particular model. A position that appears close to the building may not align with the intended receiving area. Revisit the proposed position or receiving concept using the [roof-zone survey](/blog/roof-zone-survey-roll-forming-lift-selection/), rather than assuming extra reach or a different panel direction is available.

### Check direction before parking convenience

A convenient vehicle position is only one input. Consider which side of the site it places the material supply on, where the forming outlet points, and whether the resulting panel path crosses another work area. The exact equipment arrangement must establish which orientations are possible. A site plan cannot assume that the line reverses, swivels or feeds from either end.

For example, a hypothetical parking bay beside an eave may leave the proposed coil delivery area inside a shared vehicle lane. Moving the bay could clear the lane but change the outlet relationship. That is a choice between complete layouts, not a reason to treat the feed side as interchangeable.

## Reserve the space the complete workflow needs

### Distinguish the arrival outline from the work area

The transport outline describes a particular prepared state. The work area must account for the proposed lifting and support arrangement, access required by the approved method, and the material path. These boundaries may differ considerably, but their actual dimensions must come from the configuration documents and project design.

Draw the arrival position and proposed work position separately, even if they share the same vehicle centreline. Identify any configuration transition that affects the layout. This makes it clear whether a neighbouring stockpile is outside the parked truck yet inside space needed for the planned work. The article does not provide deployment steps or establish that a transition is permitted.

### Locate support positions against actual ground features

Show the support positions described by the proposed configuration, including outriggers where applicable, against the site survey. Overlay visible covers, drainage routes, nearby excavations, repaired areas and known underground structures. A clear paved rectangle does not establish that every point within it has the same supporting construction.

Send the equipment-specific support information and the corresponding ground locations to the appointed specialist. The [support review guide](/blog/crawler-roll-forming-lift-ground-support-review/) explains the distinction between equipment inputs and site capacity, although the actual truck support cases must come from the truck configuration. Do not import crawler data or divide vehicle mass between visible legs.

### Give material delivery its own space

Draw where incoming coil or other material is received, where it is staged under the project arrangement, and how the feeding interface connects to the forming line. Keep the delivery vehicle envelope and handling operation separate from the forming equipment footprint. Otherwise the plan may reserve space for stored material while overlooking the space needed to deliver it.

Use the [coil handling and feeding guide](/blog/coil-handling-roll-forming-line-feeding-plan/) for material identity and feed coordination. On the setup drawing, the useful question is whether material supply can coexist with the proposed equipment and receiving zones. The loading method, lifting accessories and operating controls require their own configuration and site review.

### Make utilities and people visible

Locate the intended power supply, control positions and required access paths according to the proposed equipment information and site method. A temporary cable crossing or an obstructed control location can make a superficially clear layout impractical. Show the boundary with pedestrians, other plant and emergency access so the site team can decide how those uses will be managed.

The <a href="https://www.hse.gov.uk/workplacetransport/information/safeworkplace.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">HSE workplace-transport guidance</a> discusses route layout, pedestrian separation and temporary routes that change during construction. It provides general Great Britain planning context. It does not approve this equipment, classify its lifting arrangement or replace the destination site's requirements.

## Check a proposed truck setup on one layout

### Compare physical boundaries together

Use a common site drawing for the proposed truck position, support locations, material arrival and roof receiving area. A separate drawing for each contractor is useful only when the interfaces can be compared on the same coordinates and construction stage. The following table identifies what each boundary contributes; it is not a completed site assessment.

| Layout feature | Information to put beside it | Decision it supports |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Forming outlet | Controlled outlet reference, direction and required height datum | Whether the proposed position meets the receiving concept |
| Vehicle and lift | Arrival and work-state arrangement references | Which spaces change between delivery and production |
| Support area | Configuration-specific locations and site evidence references | Which ground or structural review applies |
| Material supply | Delivery position, staging boundary and feed interface | Whether supply conflicts with the reserved work area |
| Roof receiving zone | Receiving owner, geometry and current roof-work revision | Whether production has a defined destination |
| Shared access | Traffic, pedestrian and emergency routes | Which activities need different space or timing |

### Collect the inputs that can change the position

- Mark the required forming-outlet height, roof geometry and receiving reference on the same elevation.
- Identify the proposed chassis and transport state, including separately arriving forming modules or attachments.
- Plot the access route and the turn into the proposed position, with the current site traffic arrangement.
- Locate ground or floor features under the intended support area, including underground records still missing.
- Attach the controlled outrigger or other support arrangement applicable to the proposed equipment state.
- Show coil, panel material and feed staging boundaries alongside the delivery vehicle's planned access.
- Locate the proposed voltage supply, power connection and control access that affect the equipment position.
- Identify the weather and wind decision owner and the project method that governs changes to the work arrangement.
- Record the destination documentation requirements and who resolves conflicts between the equipment and site drawings.

### Record a useful conclusion

The layout conclusion should identify the constraint that still determines the position. “Outlet alignment unresolved” tells the team to revisit the equipment-to-roof relationship. “Material delivery occupies the support zone” calls for a spatial or scheduling decision. “Underground construction unknown” calls for site evidence and specialist review. These conclusions lead to different actions.

Keep a surveyed dimension separate from its acceptance. A measured lane width remains a useful fact even when the vehicle arrangement is not yet available. Equally, a confirmed equipment outline does not close an unknown support condition. Procurement can then see what is known without treating a partly completed layout as permission to begin work.

## Compare arrangements as the site changes

### Compare proximity with usable space

Locating equipment nearer the receiving area may simplify one part of the panel path while reducing room for support, access or material supply. A position farther away may provide a less congested ground layout but require a different receiving concept that the equipment and project may not support. Neither distance alone decides the better arrangement.

Compare this trade-off using each alternative's complete combination of outlet location, configuration, support area and material flow. Do not compare the best feature of one sketch with an assumed feature of another. If a required geometry is unavailable, keep that alternative provisional until the issuing party answers it.

### Test the next construction stage

Consider a hypothetical layout that is clear when the truck arrives but will share its material lane with façade deliveries the following week. The dimensions have not changed; the allocation of space has. The site team must decide whether the sequence changes, another supply location is reviewed, or the proposed setup is no longer suitable.

Check withdrawal as well. Installed cladding, scaffolding or stored panels can change the departure route. A successful arrival does not resolve that later condition. Record which construction milestone requires the layout to be revisited, without prescribing a vehicle movement or assuming a particular dismantling option.

### Recognize an arrangement that may not fit

A truck-mounted forming-lift concept may not fit where the available position cannot connect a supported equipment arrangement to the roof receiving area, or where material supply and essential site access cannot be reconciled. Unknown ground conditions, missing configuration drawings or an unreviewed roof interface prevent a defensible selection. They do not become acceptable because the truck can park nearby.

Consider another position or a different overall production route with the project team. The comparison may include ground-level forming and a separately reviewed panel transfer, but no alternative should inherit an approval from the rejected layout.

## FAQ

#### Is truck access enough to confirm the setup?

No. Vehicle arrival, the proposed work-state space, support conditions, material supply and the outlet-to-roof relationship are separate checks. A clear gate establishes none of the other four by itself.

#### Should the truck always be as close to the roof as possible?

No universal distance applies. Review the complete arrangement using the actual equipment geometry and receiving concept. A closer position may introduce a support or access conflict that a simple plan view hides.

#### Can material delivery use the equipment's support area temporarily?

Do not assume the areas can overlap. The site team and equipment parties must assess the specific timing, equipment state and approved method. Show the proposed overlap explicitly so it can be resolved.

#### Does this review decide which chassis to buy?

No. It supplies site constraints to equipment selection. Chassis integration needs the vehicle and lifting-equipment interface information; the jobsite layout then uses the resulting configuration to assess the proposed position.
