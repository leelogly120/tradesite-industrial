Choose a pre-cut roof-panel line when its sheet-feeding arrangement handles the complete order-length range and the accepted panels meet your end-quality requirements. Choose post-cut when the production brief requires cutting after the profile has formed, including short parts that the proposed pre-cut line cannot process. Compare both against actual orders before paying for a faster cutting system. Cut location and production speed are separate purchasing decisions.

A former with an attractive running speed is still the wrong purchase if it excludes the shortest saleable panel or requires an unpriced second operation. Begin with the panel schedule you sell, including the awkward lengths and small orders. Then compare the equipment that completes that schedule.

## The cut changes the material entering the rolls

In a pre-cut arrangement, the strip is cut before the forming stations. The forming section therefore receives individual lengths. In a post-cut arrangement, material passes through forming before the output is cut. That distinction changes the feed, cutting and receiving equipment that belongs in the offer.

These are established equipment categories. [Bradbury's panel-equipment overview](https://www.bradburygroup.com/products/metal-building-and-roofing-manufacturing-equipment) lists pre-cut and post-cut lines separately, alongside flying and stop-to-cut shears. [Schlebach's small profiling-machine range](https://schlebach.de/en/products/profiling/mini-profiling-machines/) also distinguishes sheet-fed equipment and a model with cutting before profiling. Those examples establish the process choices; the purchase still needs a proposal for your specific panel.

The relevant question is where the accepted product becomes an individual piece. For pre-cut, include the transfer and guidance of each cut length through the proposed former. For post-cut, include the cutter's relationship to the formed cross-section and the discharge of the finished piece. A quotation ending at the rolling stations leaves that comparison unfinished.

The tool above illustrates why the cutting arrangement needs its own identification. Put the offered cutting tool beside the controlled panel drawing in the quotation. For multiple profiles, list which tools, settings and change activities each proposal includes. Do not infer a common cut-tool scope from a common machine frame.

## Let the shortest order challenge the offer

A supplier's normal demonstration length is not necessarily your limiting product. Include the shortest ordered panel, the longest run and a representative intermediate length. Add the material and profile combinations that change the purchase, rather than a long list of nominal products with no committed demand.

For each length, require an offered feeding-to-discharge sequence and an agreed sample inspection. The supplier provides the machine limits; the buyer provides the order mix and acceptance definition. Reject a route that leaves a required length outside its offered scope unless a separately priced process covers that part of the business.

Use this worksheet for the two complete proposals. Enter actual values in the final column before ranking prices.

| Decision item | Pre-cut proposal must address | Post-cut proposal must address | Owner, buyer entry and decision use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Length range | Guidance and discharge of each cut sheet | Formed-piece cutting and discharge | Sales planner: enter shortest ___, longest ___ and quantity by length ___ to identify excluded orders |
| Panel ends | Finished end condition after feeding and forming | Finished end condition after profile cutting | Product owner: enter drawing revision ___ and end criteria ___ to define accepted output |
| Profile changes | Feed and forming changes for each product | Forming changes plus relevant cutting tools | Equipment supplier: name included tools ___ and change tasks ___ to compare full scope |
| Short batches | Setup, first accepted piece and remaining material | Same complete order sequence | Production lead: record trial batch sizes ___ and accepted pieces ___ to compare useful output |
| Receiving area | Proposed cut-sheet and finished-piece movements | Proposed output and removal arrangement | Workshop planner: enter available length ___, width ___ and removal route ___ to check the whole cell |
| Purchase cost | Line, tooling, feeding, receiving and support | The same completed production boundary | Buyer: enter complete quoted totals ___ and exclusions ___ to select the purchase |

Do not average away an excluded product. If most orders fit but a recurring short piece needs another process, price that process and the material transfer. The resulting mixed route is a legitimate comparison. Calling the main machine universal is not.

## Flying cutting belongs inside the order trial

Flying and stop-to-cut describe how the proposed cutting process operates relative to material movement. They do not replace the pre-cut versus post-cut decision. Request the supplier's actual operating sequence for the configuration being quoted; avoid assuming that every combination appears in its range.

Buy the additional cutting system only when its accepted order output addresses a measured production requirement. Count the time from order setup through the last accepted panel, including changes and interruptions. A result from a long uniform run cannot stand in for a shop selling many small batches.

Keep receiving and packing in that trial. If the outlet waits for finished panels to leave, a faster cutting cycle has not resolved the work that is holding the order back. The [production bottleneck guide](/blog/roof-panel-roll-forming-production-bottleneck-planning/) explains how to locate that wider constraint before expanding the line.

That visible boundary belongs in both quotations. Obtain the receiving arrangement, occupied space and removal scope with the cutter and former. For an elevated installation, pass the complete proposed assembly to the lift supplier; cut location alone says nothing about suitability for mounting on a lift.

## Two purchases that fail their own order book

These illustrative purchasing mistakes show how an apparently competitive line loses its advantage.

**Failure case: the recurring short panel is omitted.** A buyer compares demonstrations using long panels, then discovers that an important short product is outside the selected feeding arrangement. A second operation and transfer now sit outside the original price. Include the shortest committed order in the trial and price any exception before choosing the line.

**Failure case: the faster cutter waits at the exit.** A shop orders the higher-output cutting option, but its receiving area still fills while packs are removed. The paid-for improvement addresses a stage that was not limiting accepted output. Compare the complete order cycle and include the exit-cell purchase needed to use the proposed cutting capability.
