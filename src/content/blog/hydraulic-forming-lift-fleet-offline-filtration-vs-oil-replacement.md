**Buy a matched offline fluid-conditioning programme when analysis identifies removable contamination, the fluid remains chemically serviceable for the equipment, and repeated fleet work justifies the complete unit and service resources. Replace incorrect or degraded fluid through the equipment-specific service plan. Correct the contamination source before funding either route.** Start with identified samples and the machine's applicable fluid requirements; buying a filter cart before that assessment risks buying the wrong treatment.

This is an ownership purchase for a fleet already in service. The decision concerns conditioning equipment, competent service and replacement-fluid work. Where eligible work is infrequent, buy the defined conditioning service instead of a fleet unit. This ownership decision follows the original hydraulic design and the allocation of lift and former power units. A shared lifting package is not evidence that all its circuits use the same fluid or permit the same external service arrangement.

Where the owner's yard also contains personnel platforms, keep those assets separately identified. The low deck below shows another hydraulic equipment context within which service access, release from work and machinery identity matter. Shared conditioning resources require an asset-by-asset eligibility list, rather than one generic crawler heading.

## Cleanliness and fluid condition answer different questions

[Pall's discussion of hydraulic-fluid contamination](https://www.pall.com/en/solutions/contamination-control.html) distinguishes the fluid's base stock and additive package, and describes damage associated with particles and water. It also explains additive depletion. For procurement, that distinction means retaining the chemical-condition assessment alongside the particle result before authorising continued fluid use.

Have the laboratory and maintenance lead assess the identified fluid against the actual equipment requirements. The record should separate particle findings, water findings and chemical or physical condition. Keep the sample location, date, machine identity, fluid identity and relevant operating history attached to the interpretation. A report without those references is weak evidence for buying a fleet-wide treatment asset.

Choose conditioning when the accepted assessment identifies a removable problem and the treatment provider documents the appropriate function. Choose replacement when the fluid is wrong, chemically degraded or rejected for continued use under the applicable service decision. Include the equipment's required follow-up work with replacement; fresh fluid is not a purchased repair for an unresolved source of contamination.

Do not adopt another machine's numerical cleanliness limit or oil-change interval. The relevant acceptance belongs to the actual machine, fluid and service arrangement. The buyer's useful deliverable is an identified assessment and a funded response, rather than an invented fleet-wide maintenance frequency.

## A particle filter is not every conditioning process

[HYDAC's filter-systems description](https://www.hydac.com.au/products/filter-systems.html) separates condition monitoring from conditioning and lists filtering, dewatering and degassing functions. It also distinguishes mobile and stationary systems. These categories show why the words offline filtration are insufficient to select a purchase.

Give the provider the assessed contamination and fluid identity. Obtain the proposed removal function, compatible operating conditions, consumables, monitoring and acceptance evidence. A unit offered for particles should not receive credit for water removal unless that function is actually included and documented. Likewise, removal of ageing products is not itself proof that the fluid's original chemical condition has been restored.

The equipment provider identifies the permissible service interface and applicable machine restrictions. The conditioning provider defines its compatible unit and service scope. Competent maintenance personnel establish the actual work arrangement. Procurement should buy those responsibilities and documents, without inventing connection points, flow settings or hose-routing instructions.

## Shared-unit ownership must beat a complete service alternative

For a fleet with repeated eligible events, compare owning the conditioning unit with contracting the same defined work. Include analysis, mobilisation, consumables, competent labour, reporting, machine release and the unit's own care. An owned cart still consumes these resources. A small or dispersed programme deserves a service quotation before an equipment order.

Keep separate fluids and machine requirements identifiable in the proposal. Ask the service provider for its cross-asset contamination-control and segregation scope. A convenient shared unit loses its value if the owner's actual fleet cannot be serviced within its documented compatibility or if transfers displace other essential work.

Compare actual events over the same buyer-approved planning period. Replacement costs include the specified fluid, competent work, required checks and the quoted waste route. Conditioning costs include the complete treatment and its acceptance evidence. Add ingress correction to both alternatives where the same fault must be addressed. Neither column should win by leaving that work unpaid.

The workshop photograph illustrates a practical calendar constraint. Maintenance needs a released machine and an accepted work area, even when a unit is already stored in the owner's yard. Price the service around the equipment's actual availability; do not assume that a parked machine is ready for fluid work.

## Two hypothetical fluid-care mistakes

**Mistake (hypothetical): a water problem receives a particle-only purchase.** A fleet buyer orders a convenient filter cart after seeing a particle count but overlooks the laboratory's water finding. The delivered unit's documented scope does not address that problem. Return to the full assessment and purchase the matched treatment or replacement service required for the actual fluid condition.

**Mistake (hypothetical): a clean count becomes permission to keep degraded oil.** An owner records a better particle result after treatment and cancels the proposed fluid replacement, although the chemical assessment still rejects continued use. Keep the laboratory’s chemical rejection beside the improved particle result and follow the equipment-specific replacement decision; do not cancel that work from the particle count alone.

## Eight attachments for a supported fleet programme

1. Fleet asset identities and actual hydraulic-system boundaries.
2. Applicable equipment fluid specifications and service documents.
3. Identified laboratory results and competent interpretations.
4. Contamination-source findings and required corrective scope.
5. Eligible service interfaces and provider compatibility responses.
6. Actual service-event history and planned machine-release windows.
7. Conditioning-unit, analysis, consumable and labour quotations.
8. Equivalent replacement-fluid and waste-service quotations.
