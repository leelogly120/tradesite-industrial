A building with several eave levels needs a **zone-by-zone equipment question**, even when the buyer hopes to use one roof-forming lift. The lifted object is a compatible roll former, and the useful height concerns its panel outlet relative to a particular roof receiver. Different eaves can also have different approaches, ground levels and panel directions. The greatest roof-height figure does not contain those relationships.

This article develops a roof-zone schedule showing which questions can be grouped and which need distinct configuration responses. Use the [roof-zone survey guide](/blog/roof-zone-survey-roll-forming-lift-selection/) for collecting the general site information; the narrower task here is comparing several named zones without losing their differences.

## Divide the roof into comparable tasks

### Use a receiving zone rather than a building name

A warehouse extension, lower annex and principal hall may share one address yet create three different receiving tasks. Name each zone by a recognizable grid or workface location. Describe the first point intended to receive the emerging panel, rather than substituting the ridge or the finished panel's final position. If a long roof area has the same nominal eave level but another approach or receiving direction, give that part its own row so the equipment response can address it explicitly.

### Keep the forming requirement with the zone

Put the relevant panel profile, material reference, intended length schedule and output direction beside each zone. This is not an instruction to choose different forming equipment for each area. It prevents an assumed common panel requirement from concealing a change. The lift and former are considered together: a machine suitable for one profile is not, by that fact, confirmed for a different roof-panel task. Where the same confirmed line is proposed throughout, identify that common basis once and record the zone-specific differences.

### Distinguish shared inputs from shared capability

Several rows may use the same supplier drawing, ground survey or project panel reference. Those shared inputs reduce repeated document collection, but they do not prove that one equipment arrangement serves every row. Keep the proposed configuration response visible for each task. An offer saying all roofs included should identify how its stated scope relates to the named zones. The buyer needs a traceable equipment comparison, not an implication that the highest nominal eave encompasses all lower and differently positioned workfaces.

## Keep levels tied to their locations

### Connect local ground and roof references

The receiving elevation belongs to a project datum, while an equipment proposal may refer to the surface under its supports or another stated reference. If the lower roof is approached from lower ground, its apparent advantage can change when the actual reference points are connected. Ask the survey and equipment parties to reconcile the references rather than subtracting labels from unrelated drawings. The [height-datum guide](/blog/roll-forming-lift-height-specification-datum/) explains why platform, outlet and personnel working heights need separate names.

### Record the direction into each roof zone

Two eaves at different levels can face the same lane, or equal-level eaves can face opposite sides of a building. Put the candidate base location and the desired panel direction beside each receiving point. A configuration answer for one side should not be copied to another merely because the roof elevation matches. Columns, bracing and a projecting annex may change the relationship. The schedule should identify the drawing view that supplies those details, rather than attempting to compress the entire plan into a height column.

### Include the construction stage that creates the space

A lower annex might be accessible before facade work, while the main hall's lane is available later. Describe those expected stages separately. A schedule of roof geometry without its availability assumptions can suggest a shared position that never exists at the same stage of construction. Great Britain's <a href="https://www.hse.gov.uk/workplacetransport/information/safeworkplace.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">HSE workplace-transport guidance</a> addresses changing temporary routes and obstructions. It supports keeping site context current, without establishing a movement method or approving a forming-lift position.

## Work through a three-zone comparison

### Start with a deliberately incomplete common offer

Imagine a **hypothetical** tender for a principal hall, a lower annex and a canopy roof. Offer A names one forming-lift arrangement and quotes a single height description. The offer contains no zone-specific outlet drawing. Offer B responds to the hall and annex separately but excludes the canopy until its receiver is defined. Neither description alone selects equipment. A has broader wording with an unclear geometric basis; B has a narrower stated answer and an exposed missing task. The buyer should compare those differences rather than rank offers by apparent coverage.

### Ask what changes from one row to the next

For the hall, the missing input could be the exact cutter-to-receiver relationship. At the annex, the receiver is known but the proposed base is at another ground level. At the canopy, the project has not selected where the first panel is received. The table turns these into distinct follow-up questions at each equipment interface. A single request for additional height data would leave the canopy's upstream problem untouched. Conversely, requesting a new roof survey everywhere would waste effort where the actual gap is the supplier's configuration drawing.

### Group only after the response explains the common basis

If a supplier later identifies the same arrangement for two zones, retain both task rows and link them to that response. Grouping is then a statement about the actual proposed scope, not a guess from similar roof shapes. A remaining exclusion should stay visible when the price is discussed. The practical outcome is a short explanation of which tasks the offer addresses and which are still unanswered; it is not a plan for moving equipment between those tasks.

| Hypothetical roof task | Information already available | Unanswered comparison |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Principal hall receiving bay | Named panel and receiving elevation on the project grid | Which outlet point on the proposed arrangement corresponds to that receiver? |
| Lower annex beside another ground level | Roof section and proposed local stand area | How do the equipment's ground and outlet references relate at this position? |
| Canopy with an undecided first receiver | Intended panel task and roof area | Which receiving location must the project define before an equipment response is meaningful? |
| Another hall bay at the same eave level | Similar elevation but a different panel direction | Does the actual arrangement address the second direction and intervening structure? |
| Later annex phase | The original survey and a changed facade stage | Which location or space assumption needs updating before retaining the earlier response? |

## Ask how one proposal addresses several zones

### Make the zone schedule usable by bidders

Keep the common project references above the schedule, then give every changed relationship a concise row. Ask each bidder to respond by zone, naming its configuration basis and any excluded receiving or site work. A repeated drawing reference is acceptable when it genuinely describes multiple tasks. A repeated assertion of suitability without the relevant references does not answer the schedule. The buyer should be able to identify a missing zone or a changed assumption directly from the response without interpreting an equipment brochure.

- Give each roof receiver a grid or location label that appears on the matching drawing.
- Preserve outlet height, roof elevation and local ground reference as separate inputs for each proposed base.
- Associate the actual panel material requirement and outlet direction with every task row.
- Identify the access route and construction stage that make the candidate position available.
- Request the equipment arrangement and outlet datum that answer each named relationship.
- Keep exclusions, unresolved receivers and changed zone requirements visible in the commercial comparison.

<!-- audit-section: limitations-not-fit -->
### Do not turn the schedule into a relocation sequence

One proposed configuration may not fit every zone merely because its height label exceeds the roof levels. An answer covering several roof tasks does not establish how equipment moves between them or the state in which it may move. That separate problem belongs in the [work-zone relocation review](/blog/crawler-roll-forming-lift-work-zone-relocation/). The schedule also cannot establish support suitability, panel-support design or a universal setting for several heights. If the offer lacks enough configuration information to connect a zone to its receiver, preserve that uncertainty. A model name alone is not a reason to close the row.

## FAQ

#### Can the highest eave define the whole purchase?

It identifies one vertical requirement only when its measurement reference is clear. Other zones can change the base position, receiving direction, ground level or panel task. Ask how the actual proposed arrangement answers those relationships. The schedule keeps the largest nominal height from concealing a different lower-roof constraint, and does not claim that any specific machine can serve all levels.

#### Do equal heights belong in the same row?

Only if grouping preserves the information needed for the equipment comparison. Equal receiving elevations can coexist with different approaches or orientations. Keep a separate task where a changed condition needs its own supplier response. The purpose is to avoid losing a decision-relevant difference, not to create a separate document for every identical part of an otherwise common workface.

#### Should a partial zone response be rejected immediately?

First identify what is actually unanswered. A clearly bounded offer may be easier to develop than one claiming complete coverage without its geometric basis. Request the missing response and compare the resulting complete scopes. This article does not decide a tender outcome; it helps the buyer distinguish a known exclusion from an unsupported assumption hidden inside broad wording.

#### Does zone coverage include panel receipt and installation?

Only where the actual scope expressly includes those functions. A forming-lift response can describe where the former is positioned while leaving external support and receiving work to the project. Carry those boundaries into the [handover-zone discussion](/blog/elevated-roll-forming-roof-panel-handover-zones/) rather than treating equipment coverage as a complete installation service or a method for all roof tasks.
