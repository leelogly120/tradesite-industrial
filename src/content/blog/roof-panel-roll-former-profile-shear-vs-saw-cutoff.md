Start with a profile-matched shear for thin, open roof-panel sections when a representative trial preserves the required cut end and seam detail. Select a saw route when the proposed shear fails the specified end condition on the actual material or section, and the complete saw package passes with its cleaning and finishing included. Choose between dispatchable panels, rather than two ways of separating metal. The next step is an end-sample trial tied to the sold product drawing.

Keep two earlier choices fixed: [cutting before or after forming](/blog/roof-panel-roll-forming-pre-cut-vs-post-cut-line/) and [stop-to-cut or flying motion](/blog/roof-panel-roll-former-stop-to-cut-vs-flying-cut/). This comparison addresses the physical cutting process. For a formed-section cutoff, the supported panel contour and the resulting cut end become central purchasing inputs.

## Begin with the roof detail at the panel end

Take the controlled cross-section and mark the end features that affect assembly. Include the mating seam, exposed cut edge, any end preparation required by the roof detail and the product's dimensional and appearance criteria. A cutter passing the overall length check still fails the purchase if its end prevents the specified seam or closure detail from being assembled.

The product authority defines these criteria. The quality lead identifies where and how they will be measured. The equipment designer supplies the proposed cut support and tool arrangement. Put an accepted sample or an agreed measurable requirement beside each critical feature; “clean cut” leaves too much open for an equipment order.

For each trial, retain both ends of identified pieces where the process produces different leading and trailing conditions. Label the material lot, drawing revision and operating configuration. Inspect the end before any secondary work, then again at its dispatchable condition. This reveals which operations belong to the purchased route.

## What each cutting process adds to the line

BlueScope's historical [TB-F5 roll-forming bulletin, section 2.3](https://www.steel.org.au/getattachment/fdd37c9d-5fd4-48b0-9633-ea4fee360c91/TB-F5-Roll-forming-steel-strip_BSL.pdf) distinguishes saw cutting for thick feed or closed sections from shearing arrangements for open sections, and identifies deburring after sawing. Use that process distinction to screen proposals, then use the offered machine documents for its actual material and section limits.

For a shear proposal, identify the contour-supporting tools, the cutting action and any removed slug. Tie every tool to the exact panel revision. On a multi-profile line, identify which die, support or clamp changes with the section and which parts stay in place. Straight or swing shearing and a slug-producing cutoff create different waste boundaries; obtain the actual arrangement before assuming “shear” means a waste-free cut. [Formtek's tooling and die overview](https://www.formtekgroup.com/solutions/roll-tooling-and-dies/) identifies application-specific cutoff die and hydraulic die/press systems within roll forming, reinforcing the need to purchase a defined tool package.

For a saw proposal, include the blade, panel support and clamping, guarded working area, debris collection, receiving support and required finished-end work. Record the actual kerf and retained cleaning work from the trial. Choose the offered blade and process approved for the specified coated material, rather than treating a saw and an abrasive disc as interchangeable equipment.

[BlueScope's swarf bulletin](https://cdn.dcs.bluescope.com.au/download/tb-05) explains that loose steel particles left on coated surfaces can corrode and stain them. Its cutting guidance distinguishes cold cutting from abrasive-disc cutting. The procurement consequence is practical: retain debris control and surface inspection within the accepted cutoff cell, including the area where the next panel is received.

## Compare one clean, complete order

Run both proposals against the same product, trial material and order lengths. Start at the agreed ready state and stop when the last accepted panel reaches the defined packing handover. Include tool changes, cleaning, finishing, inspection, rejected ends and replenishment of the actual cutting tools.

Keep three cost lines visible: consumed material, labour and equipment support. Derive material consumption from the issued strip and accepted output. Record finishing and cleaning time separately so the buyer can see whether an apparently quick cut transfers work to another person. Include the actual blade or die replacement basis from the offer rather than inventing a universal tool-life figure.

If the shear trial meets every required end condition and serves the order mix at the better complete cost, retain that route. If it damages a necessary feature, compare a revised supported shear package with the complete saw route. Purchase the saw when that route passes the product trial and its retained operations fit the dispatch window. A poor first sample is grounds to correct or reject that specific proposal, rather than a universal verdict on all shears.

## Two hypothetical cutoff mistakes

**Mistake (hypothetical): the saw quotation stops at separation.** A buyer compares its price with a shear cell that includes a clean packing handover. The saw trial leaves particles and end finishing for an unbudgeted station. Add that station, labour, removal route and inspection to the saw proposal, then compare complete accepted orders.

**Mistake (hypothetical): a flat-strip sample accepts a formed seam.** A buyer approves a shear from a flat coupon although the sold panel has a formed mating edge. The first product trial produces an end outside the seam requirement. Repeat acceptance on the actual section and material, with the end detail measured before award.
