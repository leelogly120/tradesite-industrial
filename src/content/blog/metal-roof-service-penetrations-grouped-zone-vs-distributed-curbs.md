Group compatible roof services into designed zones when their actual routes, maintenance positions and drainage details support that arrangement. Keep distributed openings where the equipment functions or building layout require them. Commission coordinated alternatives before releasing metal panels, then buy the complete service and roof interfaces belonging to the selected layout. Choose the grouped option only after its service routes and working access are included; retain a justified distributed opening rather than forcing an incompatible service into the group.

Grouping is useful only when it improves a workable building package. It is not a rule to place every duct and pipe in one large opening. Distributed curbs are also a legitimate design where service routes need them. Compare the resulting roof, service installation and owner access together.

## Place the services before counting openings

Start with equipment and service functions. Record each duct, pipe, cable route or other penetration, its source and destination, required connections and maintenance access. Distinguish the opening for a service from the support for the associated plant. A surface-supported unit can still need a duct opening through the covering.

Ask the mechanical and electrical coordinators to draw a grouped layout and the justified distributed alternative where both are feasible. Give the roof designer both overlays. The appointed roof designer evaluates the proposed opening positions against the actual panel seams, roof slopes, water paths, joints and compatible opening assemblies.

The roller train below represents the repeat field-sheet manufacturing portion. Each interruption on the coordinated roof plan changes the relationship between those repeated panels and bought-in details. Set the opening layout before treating the roof as one undifferentiated sheet quantity.

## A grouped zone concentrates more than the openings

A grouped zone can bring service interfaces and maintenance destinations together. Price the actual upstream and downstream service routes, supporting work and roof assembly around that zone. Include access for installing and later replacing equipment. A shorter list of roof openings is not a complete cost saving if services require substantially different routes inside the building.

Check which functions can share a coordinated zone and which need separation under their specialists' design. Preserve those requirements in the tender. The procurement team should compare accepted layouts rather than reduce the opening count independently.

The [NZ Metal Roofing Manufacturers' penetration guidance](https://www.metalroofing.org.nz/cop/penetrations/penetration-design/penetration-flashing-installation_) discusses movement at penetrations and recognises alternative design considerations where several penetrations are close together. It reinforces the need for a designed zone; it does not turn clustered penetrations into an ordinary unmodified flashing detail.

## Distributed curbs can follow the work they serve

Distributed openings are appropriate where equipment needs local service connections, internal routing would otherwise obstruct the building, or the accepted functional design requires separate positions. Each opening then needs its own identified roof interface, component supply and completion responsibility.

Price the actual number and types of curbs or flashings, installation visits, access positions and inspections. Follow the services through the roof layers and into the building. Keep the weathering, insulation and environmental-control details with the responsible designers and trades.

For repeated openings, ask whether the selected product provider can supply a controlled family of details. Repetition in procurement can simplify identification and replacement without changing required opening locations. Preserve different items where loads, services or roof conditions differ.

## Release panels by the affected roof zone

Once the layout is selected, issue a panel mark-up identifying uninterrupted runs, pieces around openings and separately fabricated components. Name the last design-release date for each service zone. For a large project, stable roof areas can proceed separately when the roof designer and installation sequence permit.

A machinery order should identify the weather-sheet geometry and material, not merely the number of rooftop machines. Keep final trimming, specialist openings and curb fabrication assigned to the actual providers. Where the line is proposed for elevated forming, give its planner the receiving positions and the installation sequence for the selected roof fields.

The elevated unit below illustrates that production equipment also occupies ground beside the building. Coordinate that occupation with deliveries of rooftop plant and specialist opening components. A grouped service zone can concentrate several trades' receiving demands in the same project period.

## Two hypothetical layout failures

**Mistake: fewer openings are credited without the changed service routes.** A grouped proposal moves separate extraction ducts into one service zone; the estimate credits fewer curbs but omits the longer internal ducts and revised supports. Price the complete accepted service routes and roof assembly on both alternatives before selecting the layout.

**Mistake: plant supports are counted as service weathering.** A contractor orders surface support frames and removes curbs from the roofing budget. Ducts and pipes still penetrate the roof. Reinstate the actual service-opening schedule and its complete details separately from the support package.

## Eight RFQ attachments

1. Service functions, equipment identities and route endpoints.
2. Grouped and distributed layout alternatives in one coordinate system.
3. Roof sections, panel seams and accepted opening details.
4. Separate plant-support and structural references.
5. Weather-sheet profile, material and finish requirements.
6. Panel marks, lengths and separately fabricated component schedule.
7. Installation releases, receiving occupation and owner access routes.
8. Destination, production location, utilities and supply owners.
