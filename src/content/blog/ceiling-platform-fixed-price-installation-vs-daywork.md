**Use a fixed-price installation package when the ceiling output, access conditions, materials and programme are defined. Use agreed daywork for bounded exploratory or changing work, with recorded resources and an explicit spending decision at each stage. Choose the charging basis separately from platform ownership: owning the equipment does not turn an undefined installation into a fixed-price job.**

A contractor buying a ceiling platform needs an estimate of how that asset contributes to future work. Some jobs pay for a completed defined result. Other work is valued from the labour, equipment and materials actually used. Compare those revenue and cost structures before assigning the platform's purchase cost to the order book.

## The priced result and the recorded resource are different units

A fixed price should state the completed work it covers: the drawing revision, measured area or item list, installation interfaces and completion criteria. It also needs stated assumptions about access, material availability and sequencing. An attractive lump sum with a large unpriced boundary is not a complete comparison.

Daywork values specified work through recorded resource use under agreed charging rules. [RICS NRM 2, section 2.5.9](https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/NRM-2_Oct2022_Update.pdf) describes labour time, materials and plant as its basis. The commercial scope must identify the applicable rates, included costs and authorisation process; do not copy a rate or entitlement from another project.

For a stable ceiling package, request a fixed-price proposal against the same defined output from each bidder. For an exploratory opening-up task, limit the first stage, record its findings and define the next package from those findings. Do not leave the whole remaining installation on an open-ended time basis merely because the first task was uncertain.

## Use one scope sheet for both offers

Ask the project estimator to complete the buyer column, then require each bidder to respond to the same inputs. Keep material purchasing responsibility explicit in both options so a pricing comparison does not accidentally become a different supply package.

| Scope input | Buyer entry to complete | Fixed-price response | Daywork response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Defined output | Enter drawing revision and measured ceiling areas by grid | State included result and completion boundary | Identify the bounded task to be undertaken |
| Quantity basis | Enter measured items and who measured them | List quantities included and change basis | Record completed quantities alongside resource use |
| Access arrangement | Enter floor-to-task dimensions, platform positions and route | List included access and mobilisation scope | State equipment charging unit and included setup activities |
| Material readiness | Enter delivery lots, release dates and supply owner | State material assumptions built into the price | Distinguish supplied materials from chargeable consumption |
| Time records | Enter site calendar and approving supervisor | State programme and agreed reporting milestones | Identify labour/equipment records and daily resource reviewer |
| Interruption boundary | Name foreseeable access restrictions and responsible parties | Identify included restrictions and priced assumptions | State treatment of waiting, travel and non-working periods |
| Changes | Name the project person issuing changes | Define how a changed scope is separately priced | Define the next spending authorisation and task limit |
| Completion evidence | Enter inspection criteria and area handover owner | Price the specified completed output | Record findings and completed work as well as hours |

The site planner owns dates and work-area releases. The estimator owns quantities and cost comparisons. The installation lead identifies the resources required for the actual method. The commercial manager establishes the charging and change process. Assign these inputs before treating a bidder's assumptions as project facts.

## Decide how the platform earns its place in the fleet

For fixed-price work, calculate the equipment cost against a realistic task sequence: transport, setup, occupied working periods and removal. Compare that cost with the installation package's available contribution after labour, material and other direct costs. A platform that remains on site through unrelated delays still occupies capital and prevents use elsewhere.

For daywork, identify what equipment time the actual agreement pays for. Do not assume that every day on site is a chargeable day or that an equipment rate also covers transport and setup. Put agreed chargeable periods beside the machine's full occupation calendar. The difference is time the ownership plan must fund through another source.

In both cases, separate a one-off project's result from the fleet investment. Use committed repeat work and the deployment calendar to assess ownership. The [one-platform versus shared multitrade sequence guide](/blog/shared-ceiling-platform-multitrade-fitout-sequence/) addresses availability across trades; the charging basis here determines how each defined package is valued.

## Two estimate failures

**Mistake: a fixed price without a released output.** A hypothetical contractor prices a ceiling from an early area estimate. Later drawings add service openings and change the installation sequence, but the contractor's equipment budget still assumes uninterrupted work. Establish the drawing and quantity baseline before pricing. Treat the later changed package as an explicit commercial decision with its own resource effect.

**Mistake: an equipment-day figure treated as full cost recovery.** A hypothetical buyer justifies a platform from its quoted daily charge multiplied by every calendar day on site. The actual agreement records only specified working periods, while transport and waiting remain outside that charge. Rebuild the forecast from the agreed paid activities and the full ownership calendar before purchasing.
