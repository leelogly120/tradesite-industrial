**Buy an engineered overhead protection package when the project team accepts a design that keeps specific activities operating beneath the defined works and the value of retaining them justifies the full installation. Choose to vacate the area when the work requires exclusion, the protection would disable the activity anyway, or relocation delivers the same business output at lower total cost.** Price personnel access for the accepted work sequence under each option.

A distribution aisle, a retail entrance and a production cell can occupy the same floor area but have very different requirements for staying open. Define the activity the owner wants to preserve. Record who uses it, during which hours, which equipment passes through it, what clearances and environmental conditions it needs, and who can authorise changes. “Keep the building open” is too broad a promise to buy.

## Specify the hours of usable space the owner is paying for

Draw the area beneath the actual ceiling tasks. Mark the occupied workstations or routes, emergency and delivery access, connections to fixed equipment and any activity outside the work boundary that depends on them. The building operator should identify the periods that are financially or operationally important. A protection package that preserves a pedestrian route might still close the only route for receiving pallets. In that case its value is the pedestrian access retained, with the redirected deliveries priced separately.

Divide the work into installing the protection, carrying out the overhead works, altering it between areas, and removing it. For each stage, the responsible project parties must state which activities have a released arrangement and which require the area to be vacant. Base the comparison on those accepted periods. Some operations must remain excluded whatever enclosure or deck is purchased. Keep those exclusions in both bids.

It illustrates the distinction between paying to protect activities below and providing a place for the refurbishment crew to work. The example concerns that project and its designed scope. ## Obtain a protection bid with an operating boundary

Have the main contractor and temporary-works team define the protection required for the specific work and the space it will occupy. Include design, structural interfaces, access for installation, checking, cleaning, inspection, changes and removal. Include separately designed separation or environmental controls where the task and the occupied activity require them. A protective deck, enclosure and personnel work platform are separately specified duties. Put a provider and acceptance owner against each one that appears in the bid.

Check whether the equipment operators and facility staff can use the building in the proposed arrangement. Will service staff still reach the equipment that must remain on? Can the operator accept the changed lighting, working area or access to stock? How does the contractor deliver materials outside occupied hours and restore the specified operating conditions before reopening? Ask the facility owner to sign the marked operating plan rather than an unqualified statement that business can continue.

For the vacant option, price the actual move. Include any temporary work area, utilities, controlled transfer of stock, business interruption, access changes and reinstatement. A line that can continue in another hall for a month presents a different cost from an immovable production process. Where part of the operation can move, divide the floor plan accordingly. Buying limited protection for one indispensable route and moving the other activities can be stronger than paying to cover the whole hall.

## Use the same room release in both equipment quotations

| Retained activity | Function, users, route dimensions and required operating hours: ___ | Facility owner defines the benefit purchased |
| Overhead work | Task IDs, sequence and required periods of exclusion: ___ | Principal contractor establishes feasible retained hours |
| Protection package | Drawing revision, occupied footprint, design duties and acceptance owner: ___ | Temporary-works team defines the purchased installation |
| Platform task | Supporting-floor datum, ceiling elevation, horizontal offsets and crew/tool space: ___ | Access planner identifies workable positions |
| Installation stages | Closures for erection, moves, inspection and removal: ___ | Site manager prices actual disruption |
| Vacancy package | Temporary location, required services, move-out and return dates: ___ | Operations lead prices the alternate business arrangement |
| Net outcome | Completed-work cost, useful operating hours retained, lost output and reinstatement: ___ | Commercial lead compares the same building deliverable |

Compare the large-deck access bid only after marking its base and work positions on the selected room-release plan. A protective structure can take the ground position proposed for the machine. A permitted platform position can also leave an overhead area behind a duct beyond the accepted personnel envelope. Price another work position or separate specialist access for that area. Never book the protection deck as a machine support or crew working surface unless the project has a separately designed and accepted duty for that use.

## Two hypothetical purchasing mistakes

**The retained route serves pedestrians but the dispatch operation stops.** A tender assigns the value of a full production shift to keeping an aisle open. The protection footprint blocks the only turning area used for outgoing loads. Recalculate the retained business value from the activity that can actually continue. Price a different route or temporary dispatch area before award.

**The owner prices the middle of the job only.** An enclosure bid keeps a customer entrance open during ceiling replacement, but erection and removal both need exclusive possession in peak trading periods. Add the agreed closure dates and alternative entrance costs. Compare the resulting offer with a single move to temporary premises for the full works period.
