Keep material in coil form when panel lengths and project quantities frequently change, provided you have confirmed forming capacity and a suitable material-handling arrangement. Hold finished panels for genuinely repeated profiles and lengths with dependable demand and adequate protected storage. Choose a mixed policy when only part of the order book is predictable. Coil inventory preserves some production choices; finished-panel inventory removes some production waiting. Neither removes every delivery constraint.

For a contractor or distributor buying a roof-panel line, the stock decision defines what the machine must contribute. A business planning rapid supply from coil needs a production slot, suitable tooling and people when the order is released. ## Decide where the order becomes specific

A coil already commits you to material attributes such as grade, coating and width. It is not interchangeable stock for every roof profile. Within a confirmed material-and-tooling arrangement, leaving the material unformed can retain choices about the panel order that will be made from it.

Once a finished panel is formed and cut, its profile and length are assigned. That can be valuable for stable repeat orders. It can also leave the wrong finished item occupying space after a project drawing changes. The useful question is not simply which item is cheaper to store, but which choices need to remain open until the customer has released an order.

Separate confirmed jobs from forecasts. Mark the point at which the project authorises manufacture, and identify who communicates revised quantities or lengths. A machine that can produce the agreed profile is still an expensive way to make obsolete stock if production starts from superseded information.

## Use an inventory-policy worksheet by product family

| Product-family input | Keep as coil when… | Hold finished panels when… | Buyer entry / confirmation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Length and quantity changes | The final schedule frequently changes before release | The same finished items have dependable repeat demand | Sales planner: records released-order revisions and stable repeat lengths to decide when to form stock |
| Material and profile commonality | The confirmed coil can serve the intended repeat jobs | The finished profile and finish are stable | Forming supplier: maps approved profile/material/tooling combinations to usable coil stock |
| Production availability | Supported capacity can respond after release | Production is committed elsewhere during the required window | Production lead: enters actual line slots and staffed shifts to test response after release |
| Storage and handling | Suitable coil storage and feed handling are available | Protected space and handling suit the finished lengths | Warehouse manager: measures storage and handling clearances against supplier storage instructions |
| Cash tied to unsold items | Unformed stock has identified alternative orders | Finished stock has a supported turnover plan | Finance lead: enters supported demand, alternate uses and holding period to assess tied-up cash |
| Delivery readiness | Forming and dispatch fit the customer window | Finished goods remove an actual timing constraint | Dispatch lead: defines promised delivery endpoint and remaining production/handling time |

Complete separate rows for exceptional colours, unusual widths or project-only profiles. Keep a coil buffer when it serves identified repeat orders with compatible tooling; buy project-only material after the specific order is released. Mixing both in a single average can hide the stock that is hardest to use elsewhere.

## A mixed policy is often the more precise answer

One practical arrangement is to reserve suitable coil for confirmed forthcoming jobs, keep a modest buyer-defined selection of genuinely repeated finished items, and procure exceptional products to released orders. Set the quantities from your order history and supported replenishment route; there is no universal stocking percentage.

Another arrangement uses finished panels for a stable local delivery business while a separate project programme forms longer or changing orders on site. That split must account for shared resources. If both routes need the same former, operator or handling equipment at once, coil inventory alone will not make the site programme responsive.

Keep the demand assumption explicit. A sales enquiry is not the same as an approved order. Label forecast stock, identify who owns the decision to buy it, and record the alternative uses that are actually available if the expected project changes. Do not treat an unconfirmed future customer as an assured outlet for surplus panels.

## Storage exposure can erase an apparent advantage

Finished panels require a suitable plan for their lengths, packaging, support and movement. Coil stock needs its own compatible storage and handling arrangements. Both should follow the material supplier's instructions and the site's assessed method. Comparing only occupied floor area misses the routes and handling resources needed to reach and move the material.

BlueScope's guidance explains that moisture trapped within coated-sheet packs can damage surfaces, including where wetness is not immediately visible. This is a reason to include protected storage and inspection in the stock decision, rather than assuming finished goods can wait indefinitely at a jobsite. ## Failure cases that change the equipment brief

**Failure 1 — plenty of coil, no available production slot.** A distributor keeps the correct material but schedules its only compatible line for another job. A new order cannot be supplied within the intended window. Choose a finished-goods buffer for genuine repeat products or change the supported production plan; more coil does not solve that bottleneck.

**Failure 2 — the finished buffer has the wrong lengths.** Panels are made before the final project schedule is released. Revised dimensions leave stock with little immediate use, while new production is still required. Keep uncertain orders at a less committed stage and establish a clear manufacturing release before cutting the project batch.
