Choose one shared large-deck platform when ceiling, services and wall tasks can follow a realistic sequence through released work zones and each task fits the reviewed platform configuration. Arrange separate access when crews must work at the same time in different zones, use incompatible work positions or cannot reliably release the deck to the next trade.

The lowest equipment count is not always the lowest project cost. A shared platform becomes expensive when a ready crew waits for an unfinished preceding task, or when every change of trade requires a new setup and material arrangement. Compare the installed-work sequence before choosing the number of machines.

## Plan around ready work, not booked hours

A reservation is useful only when the work zone is ready. Before a trade takes the platform, the preceding work, materials, approved task method and any required service isolation must be ready too. Otherwise the machine is occupied without producing the intended work.

Plan the sequence by zone and task. For example, where the design places services above a closed ceiling, schedule the services and their checks before closing that zone. This is a planning illustration, not a universal construction sequence: the project team's actual design and work packages determine the order.

The broad deck in the next photograph suits a discussion about a shared work face. The visible space beneath it also matters. Stage each trade's materials and define access to the zone so the next crew does not inherit an occupied floor and an unprepared task.

| Wall or perimeter work | Clear wall approach and prepared materials | Wall position and clearance: measure on site | Work area clear for the next task | Wall supervisor: enters floor-to-task height, wall offsets and released approach for the wall configuration |
| Change of trade or zone | Previous crew has released equipment and floor | Reviewed movement and task setup | Named person accepts handover | Coordination lead: enters transition duration and named handover owner to test the shared calendar |

Use one row per actual task, not one row per subcontractor. A single trade can need different platform arrangements in different parts of the building. Sharing is viable only where the approved equipment and site arrangements cover those tasks.

## Price the changeovers and waits explicitly

Compare the shared-platform option with separate access over the same work period and scope. Include transport, occupation time, task setup, material replenishment and the project cost of an agreed delay. Obtain the local rates and supplier scope rather than inserting a generic daily hire price.

For the shared option, distinguish machine use from waiting. If a preceding trade overruns, does the following crew move to another ready zone, or stop? If another zone is available, can the same reviewed platform reach it without disrupting the sequence? That fallback can make sharing workable; an assumed fallback that has no route or released work does not.

For separate access, check whether the proposed machines can actually work concurrently. If the same floor zone or material route prevents two machines working concurrently, a second machine does not remove that constraint. Compare the project's available space as well as its calendar.

## When the next trade needs a different work face

A ceiling strip and a wall task can require different positions, material arrangements and clearances. Do not assume that every large deck can be reconfigured for both. The next photograph shows an upright platform arrangement beside a building; it is a reminder to draw the wall task separately and ask for the specific configuration that serves it.

![A red platform in an upright arrangement stands beside a workshop building.](/images/equipment/buyer-20261027-wall-frame.webp)
*Draw wall and ceiling positions separately before treating them as tasks for one purchased platform.*

The [IPAF site-assessment course outline](https://www.ipaf.org/en/training-courses/site-assessment-mewp-selection) identifies task, reach, tools, materials, environment and restrictions among the selection factors. Apply that task-based approach each time the planned work changes. It supports a clearer equipment brief without turning this schedule into an operating procedure.

## Two sharing plans that fail in practice

**Failure case: all trades book the same completion week.** The purchasing sheet assumes one platform because each trade needs it for only part of the programme. The detailed programme then reveals simultaneous demand in separate zones. Re-sequence the actual tasks if the project allows it; otherwise compare separate access for the overlapping work. Buying a larger deck does not create a second independent location.

**Failure case: the deck is handed over but the work area is not.** The first crew leaves material below the next work face, and an unresolved inspection prevents closure. The second crew's reservation begins while its zone remains blocked. Define both physical clearance and task readiness in the handback. Count that transition in the programme rather than hiding it inside productive hours.

These are hypothetical scheduling failures. The procurement conclusion is to purchase access for the work that can be released and completed, with enough independent capacity for genuine overlap. Keep the commercial responsibility for waits and handbacks explicit in the project agreement.

The preferred package is the one that covers the released tasks, not the one with the largest nominal platform.

### RFQ attachments for a shared-access proposal

- Work-zone plan with ceiling, service and wall tasks identified separately.
- Task sequence showing real overlap and the required completion windows.
- Work-face elevations, clearances, obstructions and measured entry routes.
- Crew, tool and material schedule for each proposed use of the platform.
- Floor information, restricted zones and available staging areas.
- Readiness and handback conditions, including the person accepting each transition.
- Power and indoor restrictions for the intended tasks.
- Destination and the equipment, transport, training or support items requested in the quotation.
