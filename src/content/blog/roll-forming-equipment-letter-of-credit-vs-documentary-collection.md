**Choose a documentary letter of credit when the seller requires an acceptable bank payment commitment for a new or less-established equipment transaction and the parties can produce its specified complying documents. Choose documentary collection when an established trading relationship, the seller's accepted credit risk and an effective cargo-document release arrangement support bank collection without that payment undertaking. Keep technical acceptance in the equipment contract under either route.** Have both banks approve the actual transaction and wording before commitment; bank document handling does not accept the machinery.

Start with a defined purchase: the former, lifting assembly, carrier or chassis, auxiliaries and destination completion work. Then ask the buyer's and seller's banks to assess the proposed transaction. Choose the instrument after both banks and contracting parties accept its actual wording and costs. Do not enter a payment method in a purchase order on the assumption that the equipment supplier offers it.

## What the bank promise actually buys

The US International Trade Administration describes a [letter of credit](https://www.trade.gov/letter-credit) as a bank commitment tied to required documents. Its [documentary-collection explanation](https://www.trade.gov/documentary-collections) distinguishes banks exchanging documents for payment or acceptance from banks guaranteeing payment. Collection is principally associated with established, trusted trade relationships. These distinctions explain the instruments; the actual banks assess the parties, country and documents for this purchase.

A letter of credit adds a defined documentary payment undertaking. It also adds document preparation, examination, fees and potential discrepancy handling. Documentary collection places a different commercial risk with the parties and needs an agreed cargo-document release arrangement. Neither label turns a quotation into a technically complete machine order.

The machine and truck in the cover image illustrate the scope question. A former resting on a vehicle is not the same purchased object as a completed integrated forming-lift arrangement. Use equipment and package identities consistently in the sales schedule, invoice description, inspection records and bank documents.

The loose shafts beside the lines make a practical point: a short machine description can hide separately supplied items. The buyer needs an agreed item schedule before the banks settle how payment documents describe the goods. Technical drawing revisions belong in the equipment records; include only the appropriate agreed references in bank documentation under professional guidance.

## Keep acceptance and document release on separate tracks

Agree the technical acceptance events before drafting payment conditions. A factory test, dispatch record, destination inspection and site integration acceptance concern different events. Assign each one a responsible issuer, a location and a measurable scope. Identify which records exist before shipment and which are only produced after destination work.

If payment is intended to depend on an inspection certificate, agree who issues it and what it certifies. Give that proposal to the banks and qualified commercial advisers before opening the credit. A condition that requires a destination record before the goods can be released creates a circular transaction if nobody has addressed access and timing.

Use the [forming-line FAT and SAT checklist](/blog/roll-forming-line-fat-sat-acceptance-checklist/) to define equipment tests. Then reconcile their actual records with the payment arrangement. The bank instrument handles its stated documents. The equipment agreement handles the supplied configuration, test criteria and remedies.

## A document list must describe a possible transaction

For split-supply equipment, map each shipment to its issuer and payment event. A locally purchased chassis will not appear in an overseas packing list simply because the overall project includes a truck-mounted lift. If the former and lifting assembly travel separately, align the permitted shipment arrangement with the agreed payment documents.

Use the same discipline for bank names, legal party names and order revisions. Let trained personnel prepare the documents. Price the cost of amending an instrument when the underlying configuration changes, and obtain the parties' acceptance of the changed equipment schedule before sending amended descriptions to the banks.

![A blue and orange line rests on timber sheets with protective film around its striped near end.](/images/equipment/buyer-20261102-wrapped-former.webp)

Film around a machine head is a visible packaging detail. Build the document description from the issued packing schedule, including package identities and contents. A photograph assists recognition; the signed technical and commercial records establish what the buyer has ordered.

## Two hypothetical payment failures

**An inspection condition has no available issuer.** A buyer asks for a certificate from its destination integrator before shipment payment, but that integrator only inspects after arrival. The banks and parties need an achievable evidence arrangement before opening the instrument. Fix the acceptance and payment sequence together rather than waiting until documents are presented.

**Collection is mistaken for bank-backed payment.** A seller dispatches a custom former believing that routing documents through banks creates the same undertaking as a letter of credit. The parties have agreed only collection. Have both banks explain the instrument and have the seller explicitly accept the transaction risk before shipment.
