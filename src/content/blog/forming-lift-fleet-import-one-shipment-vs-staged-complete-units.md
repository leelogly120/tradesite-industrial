**Ship the already committed forming-lift units together when their required in-service dates align and the destination can receive, complete and accept the whole wave. Stage independently complete units when project starts differ and each earlier wave can reach its own required work milestone with the necessary equipment and support.** First assign every purchased unit a configuration ID and an accepted-use date, then compare the delivery programmes. A partial machine arrival is not early productive capacity. Where both complete programmes meet the required dates, select the lower total for delivering the same fixed fleet.

Hold the approved fleet quantity and purchase scope fixed. This decision starts after the owner has chosen the machines and their destinations. It concerns when complete equipment systems are released for shipment, rather than whether to buy another lift, share one former between jobs or keep a speculative sales demonstration unit.

Give each unit an identity through the packing, transit, receiving, assembly and acceptance records. Several containers can constitute one complete unit; a single truck arrival can contain only part of another. Count readiness by the purchased system's actual requirements, not the number of freight documents or packages reaching the gate.

## A wave needs a complete-system contents map

The stored frames below illustrate why physical pieces and productive units need different registers. Map the actual former, lift, controls, tooling, auxiliaries and supplied documents to each committed unit. Identify buyer-owned equipment and locally supplied chassis separately. Include the resources required to finish those interfaces before the unit's work starts.

*Mark each package’s unit ID and destination completion owner before its shipment wave is released.*

A staged first wave needs every essential item for its agreed milestone. If the former, controls or required support resource is allocated to a later sailing, bring that dependency forward or move the claimed readiness date. Do not describe the first lift arrival as an independently usable production package while its essential forming system is absent.

Shared resources deserve explicit calendar entries. A commissioning team, handling equipment or buyer-owned assembly can serve successive units when the actual programme supports that sequence. It cannot be assigned simultaneously to separate sites merely because the machine quantities are already approved. Price the real transfer and repeat attendance where required.

## Match shipment releases to receiving capacity

The destination lead should identify accepted space, handling resources, competent assembly personnel and inspection appointments for each arrival window. A one-wave programme is feasible only when these resources support all the equipment arriving together. A large yard alone does not establish completion capacity.

If projects start at different times, compare holding later units at origin with receiving and storing them early at destination. Use actual offered custody, preservation, inspection and release arrangements at both locations. Keep the equipment supplier's required care attached to the unit during the selected hold. Do not assume a supplier offers free storage or a receiver has unused workshop capacity.

[DHL's industrial-projects brochure](https://www.dhl.com/content/dam/dhl/global/dhl-global-forwarding/documents/pdf/dhl-glo-dgf-industrial-projects-brochure.pdf) lists consolidation, storage, marshalling and sequencing within its logistics services. That illustrates the operations a project-logistics quotation can address. Ask the appointed logistics provider to quote storage and sequencing as named operations, with custody, release responsibilities and the actual resources it will supply.

Release dates must follow the agreed equipment completion and transport arrangements. Have the supplier identify the offered readiness of each unit and the forwarder identify its accepted shipment plan. Then work backwards from the required site milestone using those supported dates. A proposed sailing date is one event within that programme, not a substitute for it.

## Compare all committed units through their final readiness dates

For the one-shipment alternative, price the accepted transport plan, whole-wave receiving work, destination holding and the completion programme. For staging, price all waves, origin holding, repeated handling and the actual attendance required. Use the same fleet, required output and final acceptance boundary in both totals.

Separate the first useful readiness date from final fleet completion. Earlier use has value only for a real project that can accept the complete first unit. Waiting for later units is acceptable when their committed dates still fit. Conversely, splitting a shipment creates no project advantage if every site needs all units together and staging postpones an essential system.

Consider a hypothetical buyer with three committed systems and distinct project starts. The first complete system has a funded receiving and completion plan before the other projects are ready. A staged programme deserves comparison with storing the later systems at destination. If all three must serve one simultaneous start, compare a common arrival programme against the extra completion risk introduced by separate waves.

Use actual project and provider dates rather than inventing a standard transit or assembly allowance. If both complete programmes meet those dates, their itemised delivered totals decide the commercial comparison. Where one misses a required milestone, do not hide the shortfall inside a lower freight amount.

The site photograph shows the end of that chain: an equipment arrangement beside the actual work area. Keep site entry, completion checks and the release to work on each unit's programme after transport. A port arrival cannot stand in for those remaining activities.

## Two hypothetical delivery-wave mistakes

**Mistake (hypothetical): the first lift waits for its former.** A buyer stages the lifting assembly early but places its required forming line and controls in the final wave. The site reports an arrived unit while production remains unavailable. Reconcile the complete-system contents map and move the essential packages or correct the milestone before choosing that programme.

**Mistake (hypothetical): one arrival wave exceeds completion capacity.** Three already ordered complete systems arrive together, but the receiving team has booked handling and competent completion resources for only one. Equipment occupies the site while later work waits. Price and reserve whole-wave resources or stage complete systems against the actual receiving calendar.

<!-- audit-section: project-checklist -->
## Eight RFQ attachments for the fixed fleet

1. Approved unit quantities and configuration identities.
2. Unit-by-unit project destinations and accepted-use requirements.
3. Complete package map, including former, lift and essential auxiliaries.
4. Local chassis, integration and shared-resource responsibilities.
5. Offered equipment release dates and transport plans.
6. Receiving-area capacity and booked completion/inspection resources.
7. Origin and destination holding, preservation and custody quotations.
8. Whole-wave and staged-wave totals through the same fleet milestones.
