A folded-machine outline cannot describe every space question in a roll-forming lift purchase. The equipment on a separate carrier, the arrangement proposed for site movement, the supported position and the forming arrangement can contain different objects and boundaries. The buyer needs **a four-state envelope comparison for one actual offer**, not one convenient footprint copied across the whole project.

The system raises a compatible roof-panel roll former toward its receiving level. The [crawler access-route survey](/blog/crawler-roll-forming-lift-access-route-survey/) follows its arriving state. This page asks which objects and spaces the supplier's drawings represent in four separately named situations, while leaving permitted states and transitions to the actual equipment information and project review.

## Give every envelope a named state

### Separate equipment on a carrier from the machine itself

A carrier transport outline concerns the loaded vehicle arrangement being considered, not just the folded machine standing alone. Ask which assemblies and transport provisions are included in the supplied data. A crawler carried on a low-bed trailer remains crawler equipment on a separate carrier; it is not thereby a truck-mounted forming lift. The [transport-data guide](/blog/large-crawler-platform-transport-data-package/) develops that separate interface. For this comparison, name the transport object so its outline cannot silently become the width or height of the machine proposed for another state.

### Treat stowed movement as an information request

If site movement is proposed, ask the equipment party to identify the documented state and the assemblies included. Folded appearance is not permission to travel. The former, attachments and other material must be identified by the actual information applicable to the proposal, rather than included or removed by the buyer's assumption. This page does not establish that any particular state is permitted. Its purpose is to prevent a stowed outline from being used as a movement requirement without the corresponding state definition.

### Distinguish a supported position from forming work

A supported position brings another space question: where the actual support arrangement is proposed relative to the site. The subsequent forming arrangement adds the identified former and its task interfaces, including the outlet relationship. Keep those drawing references separate even if a bidder combines them on one sheet. A crawler track footprint does not describe an outrigger arrangement, and an outrigger outline does not describe the emerging-panel corridor. Naming these different spaces makes the equipment request more precise without selecting support locations or a setup sequence.

## Identify what each outline includes

### Ask for the objects inside the boundary

An envelope should name its included assembly: former, lifting structure, cabinets, identified attachments and any separate component represented in that state. An overall dimension can be misleading if one bidder includes the former while another provides only the lifting assembly. The same applies to a carrier illustration that excludes an accessory shipped separately. Ask the issuer which configuration and drawing revision its dimensions cover. Do not fill missing objects into the outline by scaling photographs or copying measurements from an apparently similar archive machine.

### Keep static outlines separate from changing space

An outline in one depicted position is not automatically a full representation of the space required through a transition or movement. Where that changing-space question matters, identify it and request the appropriate configuration-specific information from the responsible party. A corner on an access route, for example, cannot be assessed solely from a parked rectangle. Likewise a forming position should not be treated as complete merely because its final outline fits between two features. This comparison supplies no swept envelope or operating path.

### Connect every state to its actual location

Put the relevant site route, support area or roof receiver beside each state drawing. The same space may be used by other activities at different construction stages. Great Britain's <a href="https://www.hse.gov.uk/workplacetransport/information/safeworkplace.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">HSE workplace-route guidance</a> addresses obstructions, entrances and changing temporary routes. It supports describing the space in context, without defining a machine envelope or approving any transition. The buyer should distinguish a missing site observation from a missing equipment-state drawing so the right party can answer next.

## Compare an apparently compact offer

### Start with the same equipment purchase

Consider **hypothetical Offer A**, which provides one compact folded-machine outline and a general statement about working beside the roof. Offer B provides separate references for the actual carrier arrangement, a proposed movement state and a supported forming arrangement, with the external panel route excluded. Both concern the same intended panel task. No dimensions or capabilities are asserted by this example. The difference is what the supplied information describes: A's apparently compact shape does not yet answer the other states, while B's greater detail still leaves a defined external interface.

### Ask whether the compactness is a scope difference

A may exclude the former or an accessory included in B's outline. Alternatively, the two outlines may describe unlike states of otherwise similar assemblies. Ask the issuers to reconcile those inclusions before treating one figure as a space advantage. The trade-off is between a simple headline comparison and a more complete view of what occupies the actual site. More drawing detail does not itself prove fit. It lets the buyer identify the relevant unknown instead of interpreting absence from a picture as absence from the equipment.

### Use a state matrix to locate the unanswered question

The following matrix is a request structure, not a list of approved states. A bidder can identify that information is unavailable or that a requested state does not apply to its proposal. Those answers are more useful than forcing every offer into one common sketch. Keep the actual equipment and project references beside each response, so a later revision can be checked for changed objects rather than just changed dimensions.

## Request a four-state space response

### Use the same configuration identity throughout

Ask the provider to identify which drawings describe the actual offered combination and whether any state uses another assembly or excludes a component. The [crawler forming-lift family](/products/#crawler-roll-forming-lifts) introduces the architecture, while a truck-mounted offer requires its own state information. The useful outcome is not four attractive silhouettes. It is a set of attributable boundaries that can be compared with the transport, site and roof tasks without inventing a transition or assuming that a component disappears between pictures.

- Name the actual former, lifting assembly and attachments included in each state reference.
- Separate carrier transport, proposed movement, supported position and forming arrangement in the enquiry.
- Identify the outlet height and roof receiving geometry relevant only to the forming task.
- Show the access route and the ground or floor area corresponding to the requested state.
- Ask which outrigger or other support boundary is represented, without inventing its dimensions.
- State the panel material requirement and destination handoff so external equipment and project scope remain visible.

<!-- audit-section: limitations-not-fit -->
### Recognize when one outline is being asked to prove too much

An offer may not fit the intended comparison when its sole outline has no defined state, excludes unidentified objects or is reused for transport and work without explanation. Ask for the missing response rather than deriving another state from the picture. This article sets no movement permission, clearance margin, support arrangement or assembly sequence. A documented envelope still needs to be considered with the actual route, support evidence and operating information by the responsible parties; the matrix is a purchasing aid, not a method of use.

## FAQ

#### Is a folded-machine photograph a movement-state drawing?

No. It shows a visible scene without establishing the applicable permitted state or dimensions. Supports may be deployed, and the included assemblies may differ from the proposed purchase. Ask for the actual equipment information naming the state and the relevant configuration. A photograph can help explain which object you mean, but it cannot substitute for that response.

#### Does the track footprint define the working area?

It identifies one part of the machine's physical extent, not the whole supported forming arrangement. The actual support system, former and task interfaces need their own information. Do not add a guessed surrounding margin to convert a track rectangle into a working footprint. Ask which attributable drawing answers the site-space question and what remains outside its boundary.

#### Must every offer have four separate drawing sheets?

No. One coherent drawing set can contain several clearly identified states. The important point is that the reader can distinguish their assemblies, references and scope. A provider can also identify a requested state as inapplicable. The buyer needs comparable information, not a particular document count or a presentation format that creates false similarity between different arrangements.

#### Can the matrix establish the transition between states?

No. It describes the questions attached to each stated situation. Permitted transitions, equipment preparation and work methods require the actual configuration information and responsible project review. Do not infer a procedure from the order of rows. Identifying complete states helps expose missing scope, but it does not authorize movement, assembly or forming work.
