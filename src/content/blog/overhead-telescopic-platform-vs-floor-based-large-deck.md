Commission a specialist overhead telescopic platform when a defined production bay needs repeated access from above, the building can carry the complete designed system and its moving envelope serves the work. Choose a floor-based large deck when accepted ground positions provide the required working surface and the asset must also serve other locations.

An overhead system changes the building investment. A floor-based machine changes the equipment fleet. Compare the same completed work in the bay before choosing between them; neither a crane trolley price nor a mobile-platform price represents the whole installed access package.

## A moving system above the bay is different from a walkway

An overhead telescopic access system brings a working platform down from its overhead carrier. The carrier's route and the mast's working envelope determine which positions it serves. A fixed elevated walkway instead gives people a route to its designed destinations. These are different assets, even though both use building support.

[CTI Systems' manufacturer brochure](https://www.ctisystems.com/files/113694.pdf) describes teleplatforms using a telescoping mast and an overhead carrier. Its specialist aircraft-production examples establish that access architecture. They do not turn an existing factory crane into a personnel platform or make an ordinary ceiling machine an aircraft-service system.

For a new work bay, ask the specialist to show the offered carrier route, full moving envelope, access arrangement and maintenance provisions against the process layout. The building designer identifies the supporting work included in that package. Keep the design of that integrated system with its actual specialist.

The low ground-based machine below shows the alternative support path. Its base and deployed legs occupy the floor. That occupied space belongs in the proposed production layout rather than being treated as spare space after equipment is installed.

## Buy a coverage map, not just a carrier

Use the bay's actual task schedule. Separate work on a changing production object from maintenance of the building itself. State which duty each bidder is pricing, and obtain any required task-specific equipment acceptance from the responsible parties.

| Buyer input | Entry and measurement reference | Responsible owner | Effect on the order |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bay and task limits | Building grids, task positions and completed process stage ___ | Process engineer and installation lead | Establishes the coverage being bought |
| Object envelope | Actual workpiece or building-interface geometry at each stage ___ | Process owner or building designer | Tests the working platform's access to the task |
| Overhead route | Carrier path, mast envelope and end-of-route coverage ___ | Specialist system provider | Identifies uncovered positions and required infrastructure |
| Building interface | Runway/support proposal, reactions and structural references ___ | Specialist provider and structural engineer | Includes the real building work in the capital package |
| Floor alternative | Setup positions, route and complete support footprint ___ | Mobile-equipment provider and facility engineer | Admits or excludes the ground-supported arrangement |
| Production conflict | Other moving plant, operating envelopes and available windows ___ | Production manager | Establishes whether the access system can be used when needed |
| Service and exit | Inspection access, replacement route and asset removal arrangements ___ | Actual equipment providers and facility manager | Prices continuing ownership of each architecture |
| Complete comparison | Equipment, supporting work, interruption and retained exceptions ___ | Estimator using actual offers | Ranks the two complete packages |

Measure ground-based work positions from their supporting floor. Identify the overhead system's datum and relate it to the same building coordinates. A comparison that uses floor-to-task height for one bidder and a nominal mast travel for another has not described equivalent coverage.

Include the end bays and the outer edges of the work object. An overhead carrier that reaches the middle of a bay still needs a defined working position at its limits. Likewise, a ground machine placed conveniently near the entrance must serve the distant task with the actual structure and production equipment present.

## Decide what the factory is prepared to own

The overhead route purchases installed infrastructure together with access equipment. Include its structural interfaces, installation period, specialist handover and continuing service scope. Identify the work left outside its designed envelope, including tasks in other bays.

The mobile route purchases a deployable asset. Include floor occupation, delivery between work locations, storage and the resources needed for its accepted configurations. Portability has value when the business has real work elsewhere, rather than only an intention to find future jobs.

Compare the two routes over the same committed task programme. Use actual equipment and building-work offers and the factory's own occupation records. Keep production interruption visible on both sides. Do not assume that operating from overhead eliminates every floor-level exclusion or material-handling requirement.

The wall-side photograph below illustrates why a plan view is insufficient. The working frame, wall and supporting ground have a specific spatial relationship. Draw a section through equivalent difficult positions in the proposed bay.

A specialist overhead arrangement and a separate mobile package can also serve different duties. Keep that mixed scope explicit. For example, an installed system can cover the specified production bay while separately accepted ground equipment serves ceiling or wall installation elsewhere. Price the retained tasks rather than declaring the whole facility covered.

## Two incomplete overhead-system purchases

**Mistake: the tender buys the mast but omits the runway.** A hypothetical factory compares a specialist equipment line with a complete mobile-platform quotation. The overhead offer excludes structural modifications and the work needed to install its carrier route. Add those actual interfaces and installation costs before ranking the capital purchases.

**Mistake: the access carrier is booked into occupied crane time.** Another proposal assumes a shared overhead route, but the production programme requires material handling in the same envelope during the access task. A trolley connection alone has not made both duties available. Obtain a coordinated specialist arrangement, price a separately supported route or retain a different accepted access package.

These examples concern the scope of a purchased system. Any conversion of existing lifting equipment belongs with the responsible designers and specialist suppliers.
