## Introduction

A 22-ton aerial platform sounds intimidating for indoor use—until you understand ground pressure. Facility managers at airports, exhibition centers, and logistics warehouses face a common dilemma: heavy maintenance equipment is essential for reaching high ceilings, yet sensitive flooring cannot withstand concentrated loads. A single miscalculation can lead to cracked tiles, deformed concrete, or structural damage that costs tens of thousands of dollars to repair.

The key metric that bridges this gap is **ground pressure**—the force a machine exerts per unit area of contact with the floor. Unlike total weight, ground pressure accounts for how that weight is distributed. A crawler-type under-ceiling platform with wide tracks can exert less pressure than a lightweight spider lift on outrigger pads, simply because the load is spread over a much larger surface.

This guide explains how to calculate ground pressure, compare it against common floor load limits, and select the right low ground pressure aerial lift for your facility. By the end, you will have a practical framework for evaluating any indoor aerial platform against your floor conditions.

## What Is Ground Pressure?

Ground pressure is defined as the force per unit area that a machine exerts on the surface beneath it. It is measured in **kilopascals (kPa)** in the metric system or **pounds per square inch (psi)** in imperial units. The concept is straightforward: the heavier the machine and the smaller its contact area, the higher the ground pressure.

### Why Ground Pressure Matters Indoors

Every floor structure has a maximum load capacity, known as its **design load**, determined during the building's engineering phase. Exceeding this limit—even briefly—can cause:

- **Surface cracking** in polished concrete or ceramic tile
- **Permanent deformation** of wood subfloors
- **Structural compromise** of raised access flooring systems
- **Voiding of flooring warranties** that specify load thresholds

Indoor facilities often feature expensive finishes—polished concrete in airport terminals, porcelain tile in convention centers, hardwood in heritage buildings. These surfaces are particularly vulnerable to point loads from outrigger pads or narrow-track machines.

This is where crawler-type under-ceiling platforms have a distinct advantage. Their continuous track system distributes the entire machine weight across the full length and width of both tracks, producing a uniform, low-pressure footprint rather than concentrated point loads.

## Calculating Ground Pressure

### The Formula

The ground pressure calculation is simple:

```
Ground Pressure (kPa) = Machine Weight (kg) × 9.81 ÷ Contact Area (m²)
```

Where 9.81 is the gravitational acceleration constant (m/s²), converting mass to force in Newtons, and the result is expressed in Pascals (N/m²), then divided by 1,000 for kPa.

### Calculation Example: FDDPT-25M Crawler Platform

Let's apply this formula to the [FDDPT-25M crawler under-ceiling platform](/products/fddpt-25m-crawler-ceiling-platform/):

| Parameter | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Machine weight | 22,000 kg |
| Track length | 4.2 m |
| Track width | 0.36 m |
| Number of tracks | 2 |
| **Total contact area** | **2 × (4.2 × 0.36) = 3.02 m²** |

```
Ground Pressure = 22,000 × 9.81 ÷ 3.02
                = 215,820 ÷ 3.02
                = 71,463 Pa
                ≈ 71.5 kPa
```

At **71.5 kPa**, the FDDPT-25M exerts roughly the same pressure as a person standing on one foot—remarkably low for a 22-ton machine.

### Spider Lift Comparison

A typical 5-ton spider lift with four outrigger pads (each approximately 0.06 m²) generates:

```
5,000 × 9.81 ÷ (4 × 0.06) = 49,050 ÷ 0.24 ≈ 204 kPa
```

That is nearly **three times** the ground pressure of the 22-ton FDDPT-25M. The spider lift is lighter overall, but its concentrated outrigger points create much higher local pressure on the floor.

## Common Floor Load Limits

Understanding your floor's capacity is the first step in platform selection. The table below summarizes typical load limits for common indoor floor types:

| Floor Type | Typical Load Limit | Suitable FDDPT Models |
|------------|-------------------|----------------------|
| **Standard concrete slab** | 250–500 kPa | All FDDPT models |
| **Polished concrete** | 150–250 kPa | FDDPT-20M, 25M, 31M |
| **Ceramic tile** | 100–200 kPa | FDDPT-20M, 25M (with non-marking tracks) |
| **Wood flooring** | 50–150 kPa | Consult structural engineer |
| **Raised access floor** | 50–100 kPa | Not recommended without reinforcement |

### Important Considerations

- **Always verify actual floor capacity** with a building engineer before deployment. Published values are guidelines, not guarantees.
- **Dynamic loads exceed static loads.** Machine movement, turning, and braking create additional forces. Apply a safety factor of 50–70% of the rated floor capacity.
- **Temperature and moisture** can reduce the load-bearing capacity of some floor types, particularly wood and composite materials.

## Solutions for Sensitive Floors

When your floor conditions require extra protection, several strategies can further reduce ground pressure and prevent surface damage.

### Non-Marking Rubber Tracks

Replacing standard steel crawler pads with non-marking rubber tracks eliminates surface scuffing and reduces noise during operation. Rubber tracks also slightly increase the effective contact area due to their flexibility, which helps lower ground pressure marginally. This is the standard recommendation for polished concrete and tile floors.

### Wider Crawler Pads

Optional wider track pads increase the contact area by 20–30%, directly reducing ground pressure by the same proportion. For example, upgrading the FDDPT-25M from 0.36m to 0.46m wide pads would lower ground pressure from 71.5 kPa to approximately 55 kPa. The trade-off is a wider machine footprint, which may affect passage through narrow doorways.

### Load Distribution Mats

Steel or aluminum mats placed beneath the crawler tracks spread the load over an even larger area. This is particularly useful for temporary protection of sensitive surfaces during short-duration projects. Mats should extend at least 0.5 meters beyond the track perimeter on all sides.

### Selecting a Lighter Model

When floor conditions are particularly restrictive, choosing the [FDDPT-20M](/products/fddpt-20m-crawler-ceiling-platform/) (65 kPa) over the FDDPT-31M (81 kPa) may be the simplest solution. The trade-off is reduced maximum working height, but for many indoor applications below 22 meters, the lighter model provides ample reach with greater floor compatibility.

## Case Study: Airport Terminal with Polished Concrete

### Challenge

A major international airport terminal required ceiling maintenance at heights up to 28 meters. The facility featured newly installed polished concrete floors with a verified load limit of **200 kPa**. The project team was concerned about floor damage from heavy equipment, particularly given the high visibility and cost of the flooring.

### Solution

After evaluating ground pressure calculations, the team selected the **FDDPT-31M** crawler under-ceiling platform:

- Machine weight: 25,000 kg
- Ground pressure: **~81 kPa** (well under the 200 kPa floor limit)
- Safety margin: 81 kPa ÷ 200 kPa = **40.5% utilization** (excellent safety factor)
- Equipped with non-marking rubber tracks to prevent surface scuffing

A detailed ground pressure calculation report was provided to the airport engineering team for approval before deployment.

### Result

The project was completed with **zero floor damage**. Post-project inspection confirmed no cracking, deformation, or surface marking. The client approved the FDDPT-31M for all future maintenance projects at the terminal, and the ground pressure assessment methodology became part of their standard contractor qualification process.

## Pre-Project Ground Pressure Checklist

Before deploying any aerial platform on sensitive indoor floors, work through this checklist:

- [ ] Obtain floor load specifications from the building engineer
- [ ] Calculate ground pressure for the selected platform using the formula above
- [ ] Verify ground pressure is less than 70% of the floor's rated limit (safety factor)
- [ ] Specify non-marking rubber tracks for polished, tiled, or wood surfaces
- [ ] Plan travel routes to minimize distance over sensitive areas
- [ ] Prepare load distribution mats if ground pressure exceeds 60% of floor limit
- [ ] Document floor condition with photographs before and after work
- [ ] Brief the operator on speed limits and turning restrictions for the floor type
